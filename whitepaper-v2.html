Whitepaper v2 — working draft

Substrate: the compute platform for embodied AI

Frontier physical AI increasingly wants datacenter-class inference inside machines that live on tens of watts and hard real-time deadlines. The interesting question is not whether we can build a chip a few times more efficient than a GPU. It is whether that efficiency, together with model and compiler co-design, can move important robot workloads across the physical boundaries that today keep them off the machine.

Substrate does not assume that a 2–3× accelerator is intrinsically worth building. Its value depends on whether that improvement crosses a hard physical constraint and enables materially more capable intelligence onboard a robot. Our program is explicitly designed to test that question before committing substantial capital to silicon.

Source tags: [M] measured ourselves · [S] computed by our simulator · [F] measured in production at a robotics company · [X] published elsewhere · [T] a target, not yet reached.

Executive summary

Humanoid robotics is converging on video-action models that predict several seconds of motion and replan fast enough for human-like reaction latency. The leading models are still too large to run inside a robot head, so teams run them on kilowatt-class datacenter GPUs and stream the answer back over a network. That puts latency, jitter, and connectivity failures directly inside the control loop [F].

Early architectural analysis suggests specialization alone may yield roughly 2–3× more useful work per watt than a general-purpose edge GPU held to the same power [S]. We do not claim this alone runs today’s largest world models onboard — it does not. We claim something more defensible: physical-AI inference contains enough stable, exploitable structure that co-designing hardware, compiler, and model together can move important workloads from impractical to deployable.

So the paper is organized around a distinction we think matters more than any single multiplier: the difference between making a workload faster and making it possible. In edge robotics, crossing a hard constraint changes a workload from off-board to on-board, and that is where the value is.

1. The problem

A robot’s controller now runs on a large video-action diffusion transformer that has to answer within a hard control period, from inside a head that can reject only tens of watts. Neither the datacenter nor the radio is a real answer. A datacenter GPU meets the deadline at a kilowatt and a network hop away; a radio link carries the answer back until the channel gets busy, at which point jitter and loss land inside a stability-critical loop [F]. A machine that needs connectivity to stay upright is a demo, not a product. The inference has to live on the robot.

2. Physical-AI inference is structurally different

General-purpose AI inference optimizes throughput across many requests. Embodied inference does not look like that. It is batch-one, under a hard deadline, on a bounded power budget, with continual sensor input and a great deal of temporal redundancy between one observation and the next. Those differences are not incidental. They are what create room to specialize — and, just as importantly, they are properties we expect embodied AI to keep even as specific model architectures churn.

3. What are we actually specializing?

The failure mode for a silicon company is to hardwire the wrong thing. If we etch a 14-billion-parameter diffusion transformer into a mask and the field moves to a recurrent state-space model in eighteen months, the chip is scrap. So we separate the workload into what is likely stable and what is not.

Stable properties, the ones we expect embodied inference to retain across architecture changes, and therefore the ones worth building into hardware: batch-one inference; hard latency deadlines; a bounded power and thermal envelope; a continual stream of sensor input; temporal redundancy between successive observations; persistent state; multimodality; repeated inference over slowly-evolving observations; low-precision tensor math; heavy tensor movement; predictable deployment shapes; and long-running control loops.

Unstable properties, the ones we should not freeze into silicon yet: diffusion versus autoregressive generation; softmax attention versus state-space models; JEPA versus pixel prediction; exact tensor shapes; tokenization; the number of denoising steps; and any one current VLA architecture.

The architectural philosophy follows directly: hardwire the invariants, and keep programmability around the uncertainty. DreamZero is our reference workload for stressing the architecture, not the thing we specialize for. The boundary we specialize is embodied inference.

4. Efficiency is not capability: the capability boundary

A multiplier only matters insofar as it changes what the robot can do. Performance has highly nonlinear commercial value in edge robotics, because robots operate under hard constraints, and crossing a threshold turns a workload from impossible to possible. We call each such threshold a capability boundary.

Consider a workload with a 200 ms control deadline. A general-purpose part that takes 350 ms misses it; a specialized part that takes 175 ms clears it. That 2× is transformative, because it crosses the boundary from off-board to real-time on-board (illustrative). Contrast a workload where the specialized part takes 6.2 s against the same 200 ms deadline: the same 2× is real but not yet commercially sufficient, because nothing crossed a boundary.

The boundaries worth crossing are concrete: control-loop latency; thermal envelope; battery power; memory capacity; memory bandwidth; physical cooling; bill of materials; the ability to operate offline; and model quality achievable at a fixed power. Substrate does not optimize benchmark throughput for its own sake. It tries to move the frontier of what intelligence can physically reside onboard an autonomous machine.

5. The evidence so far, stated plainly

We built a simulator and made it reproduce measured Blackwell silicon to within 0.7–4.6 % on latency before letting it predict anything [M]. Under that calibration, architecture specialization at the same power buys roughly 2–3× useful work per watt [S].

Hardware specialization alone does not close the current gap. On a DreamZero-class 14 B model, the design finishes a chunk in about 6.2 s where a same-power Thor takes about 13.0 s [S] — a genuine 2.1× win, and roughly 31× short of a 200 ms deadline. We feature that number rather than bury it, because it is informative: running this model onboard under today’s assumptions requires some combination of architecture efficiency, model efficiency, temporal reuse, and algorithmic reduction.

We are deliberately careful here. Those factors do not automatically multiply into a single clean number, because their gains can overlap. We do not currently know whether sufficient combined gains exist. Determining that is one of the central technical questions of the program.

6. η is an architecture-validation metric, not the north star

We keep η, the per-joule efficiency advantage of our datapath over the calibrated baseline:

η = (useful work per joule on the RPU) / (useful work per joule on the baseline)

But it is a validation metric, not the measure of success. The program has two gates, and the second dominates.

Gate A — architecture. Mapped η must clear roughly 2.2 [T]. Below that, specialization is not producing enough advantage to justify further hardware work, and we stop.

Gate B — capability. Can the resulting system run an important physical-AI workload inside a power and latency envelope that the best realistic general-purpose edge platform cannot? If η is 2.5 and nothing important crosses a capability boundary, we do not tape out. If η is 2.5 and an important workload moves from off-board or too-slow to real-time and onboard, then 2.5× is an excellent result. This distinction governs the whole program.

7. The comparison we hold ourselves to

We do not benchmark against a naive Thor. The standard is the RPU against the best reasonably optimized NVIDIA implementation available at the same time: fused and FlashAttention-class kernels, FP8/FP4 where appropriate, quantization, TensorRT and compilation, batch-one tuning, and whatever model-side optimizations a sophisticated robotics team would actually deploy. The rule that keeps us honest: if an algorithmic or software optimization works equally well on the GPU, it does not count as an RPU architectural advantage. Otherwise the company optimizes against a straw man and learns nothing.

8. Co-design, without counting hypothetical gains

The durable company is not custom hardware for existing models. It is model, compiler, and architecture co-evolving:

model ↔ compiler ↔ architecture

Potential sources of gain include temporal reuse; recurrent or persistent latent state; KV and state compression; reduced precision; structured sparsity; distillation; token reduction; fewer generative iterations; architectures chosen for efficient inference; and sensor-to-action pipelines designed around the hardware. We will not write “2.5× × 3× × 2× = 15×” into this paper. These are independent research directions whose gains may overlap, and we will measure their joint effect end-to-end rather than multiply isolated benchmark improvements [T].

9. The strongest case against us

Here is the alternative hypothesis, stated as well as we can state it. General-purpose accelerators improve quickly enough, and robotics models shrink quickly enough, that dedicated physical-AI silicon never reaches an advantage large enough to justify the cost of leaving the incumbent ecosystem.

The specific things that could make Substrate unnecessary: models shrinking faster than we expect; better quantization; future Thor generations; a shift to state-space or recurrent architectures that are cheaper to serve; aggressive distillation; steadily improving GPU kernels; robotics staying a small compute market; and model architectures changing faster than silicon cycles. Our thesis is falsified if, after Phase 2 below, we cannot find an important workload that crosses a capability boundary on our architecture and not on the best-optimized GPU at the same power. These are the real reasons the company could fail, and we would rather name them than discover them late.

10. Why specialization sometimes wins

Specialized processors that succeed usually do more than nudge a benchmark. They either (1) deliver an overwhelming efficiency improvement, (2) cross a hard deployment constraint, or (3) vertically integrate enough of the workload that the chip becomes part of a larger system advantage. We are not betting on category 1 — we do not claim the first TPU’s enormous multiplier. We are betting on 2 and 3: edge robotics creates hard constraints that amplify a comparatively modest efficiency improvement, and model-hardware co-design increases the total system advantage over time.

11. The product

Substrate builds the inference computer for intelligent robots, not merely an ASIC. The stack the company grows into:

LayerWhat it is
Robot modelsthe world-action models a customer wants to run
Model optimization / co-designdistillation, reuse, precision, chosen for inference
Compiler + runtimeschedule, memory movement, deadline certificate
Physical-AI architecturethe dataflow and memory hierarchy we specialize
RPU siliconthe part in the head
Robotthe machine it runs in

Owning more than one layer is also what makes the business robust: if one model family disappears, the platform does not.

12. A roadmap of progressively expensive falsification

We are not starting a company whose immediate job is to tape out a robot ASIC. We are starting one whose immediate job is to determine whether a robot ASIC deserves to exist. Each phase is a chance to kill the thesis cheaply before the next dollar tier.

Phase 2 is the one most roadmaps skip, and it is the one that decides whether the rest is worth doing.

13. The open questions, and who we are looking for

We are not hiring people to build something we have already figured out. We think embodied intelligence demands a new computing architecture, we have early evidence, and many of the fundamental questions are still open. We are assembling a small team to work out what the computer inside the next generation of intelligent machines should be. The questions on the table:

  • What computation is genuinely stable across physical-AI models?
  • How much temporal redundancy can actually be exploited?
  • How much generality can we delete before we lose the ability to follow the model layer?
  • What must stay programmable?
  • Can model architectures themselves be redesigned around edge inference?
  • Where, concretely, does specialization cross a deployment boundary?
  • What is the real energy cost of moving data versus computing it?

If those read like the problem you want, the instrument, the data, and the open questions are here.
