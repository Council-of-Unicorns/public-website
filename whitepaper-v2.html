Whitepaper v2 — business plan (draft)

Substrate: the inference computer for intelligent robots

Robot control is moving to large world-action models that must run in real time inside the robot’s head. Today they cannot, so every deployed humanoid depends on a datacenter GPU and a live network link [F]. That is the highest-value socket in the machine, it is a general-purpose part today, and it is unclaimed. Substrate is building the chip that takes it.

Source tags: [M] measured ourselves · [S] computed by our simulator · [F] measured in production at a robotics company · [X] published elsewhere · [T] a target, not yet reached.

Executive summary

Our near-term product is a specialized accelerator, the RPU, that runs the same world-action models 2.9× faster than NVIDIA’s edge part at first silicon, rising to 4.2× as the compiler matures, at the same power [S]. That lead wins design-ins now: more compelling demos, larger deployable models, lower inference cost, and more of the control loop running onboard. Our long-term company co-designs the model and the silicon into a part a general-purpose GPU cannot economically match. This document is the business case, and the plan to prove or kill the bet cheaply before committing capital to silicon.

1. The market: every robot head is a socket

The compute module is among the highest-value components in a humanoid, and today that socket is a ~$3k general-purpose Jetson module [X]. A world-model-native part at a $1–2k module price, across a street-forecast of more than a million units a year (Goldman projects ~1.4 million by 2035 [X]), is a $1–2 B/year serviceable market by the early 2030s [T] — before the same silicon spreads to mobile manipulation, warehouse robots, and any embodied platform running a world model under a deadline. The wedge is sharper than the number: the socket is won per robot generation, so one design win is multi-year, multi-hundred-thousand-unit revenue with certification lock-in.

2. The problem, in business terms

Today’s world models are too large to run onboard, so teams run them in a datacenter and stream the answer back over a radio. That puts network jitter and connectivity failures inside a stability-critical control loop [F], and it does not scale: a dedicated kilowatt GPU per robot costs on the order of $30k, and shared GPUs contend on tail latency exactly when two robots need an answer at once. A humanoid that needs connectivity to stay upright is a demo, not a product. The inference has to move onboard, and the silicon to do it does not yet exist.

3. What we build

The RPU is a specialized inference accelerator for the robot head, with the model’s dataflow etched into silicon — the bet Etched made for the datacenter (and raised $800 M on [X]) and Google made with the TPU, aimed at the one place neither is looking. Around the chip we build the compiler, the runtime, and the model-optimization layer, so the product is an inference computer rather than a bare ASIC. That also makes the business robust: if any one model family disappears, the platform does not.

4. The wedge: a durable lead, not a finished chip

We do not need full onboard autonomy to build a valuable company. The near-term product holds a large, measurable lead over the best general-purpose edge platform on world-model inference — a higher refresh rate, a larger deployable model, lower power, lower latency [T]. In edge robotics that lead is worth more than the raw multiple suggests, because crossing a hard constraint (a control deadline, a thermal envelope, offline operation) turns a workload from off-board to onboard. Even before the whole model fits in the head, a purpose-built part shrinks the datacenter behind each robot, improves the economics of remote inference, and moves progressively more of the loop onboard. That is what earns the first deployments.

5. Why it stays defensible

Certification. Deterministic timing, once it is inside a customer’s safety case, is a moat measured in years, not months.

Co-design. Once model architectures settle, we design the network and the chip together and etch the model itself into the hardware — a part a general-purpose GPU cannot economically match.

Structure. NVIDIA cannot take our efficiency without giving up the CUDA generality that is their product. And efficiency does not improve on the headline curve: measured GPU performance per watt doubles only every three to four years [X], and inside a fixed-power head you cannot spend past it. So absolute feasibility arrives for everyone as models shrink and silicon improves, but the efficiency ratio we hold survives that.

7. Why now

Robotics scaling laws are emerging: Physical Intelligence has shown human-to-robot transfer once enough teleoperated data exists [X], and the field is racing to collect it. Model architectures are converging — transformers froze, which is what makes etching rational — and shrinking fast, from billions of parameters a year ago to 2.3 B, 1 B, and 500 M today [X]. Humanoids are shipping now with world-model control loops and nothing to run them on but a general-purpose SoC at the wrong operating point.

8. The bet, priced honestly

The company reduces to one number: how much less energy our datapath spends per FLOP than a GPU. Architecture specialization contributes about 2.2× on its own [S], inside the 1.6–3.2× band TPUv4 demonstrated over an A100 [X]; with the compiler and physical-design levers the working design points are 2.9× at first silicon rising to 4.2× as the compiler matures, above a 2× minimum bar and a 3× target [S]. We do not claim this alone runs today’s largest model onboard — it does not. We claim that with model and compiler co-design it can move important workloads across a deployment boundary, and the program below is built to test exactly that. We would rather name the ways this fails than meet them in diligence: models shrinking faster than we expect, better GPU kernels, robotics staying a small compute market, or architectures changing faster than silicon cycles.

9. Capital-efficient roadmap: falsify before you fund

We are not starting a company whose first job is to tape out a chip. Its first job is to find out whether the chip deserves to exist. Each phase is a cheap chance to kill the thesis before the next dollar tier.

10. Team and ask

The founding team has done the two hardest adjacent jobs and the silicon work around them: building real-time world-model inference and the remote-inference stack at a frontier humanoid company (after four years at SpaceX), AI and physics-theory R&D for semiconductor chip inspection (leaving a PhD at MIT to build full-time), and senior product for physical AI and IoT at Microsoft.

We are raising a pre-seed to run the program through Phase 3: an FPGA-measured efficiency advantage, a capability boundary an important workload actually crosses, and a design-partner LOI. That is the evidence that turns this from an exciting benchmark into a company — and the point at which the far larger co-designed system becomes worth building.
