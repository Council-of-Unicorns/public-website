The roadmap,
gate by gate

Substrate builds the RPU: an inference coprocessor module and compiler stack that let robots run substantially more onboard model inside a fixed 20–40 W envelope than general-purpose edge GPUs. The pitch says why and the whitepaper says how; this page is the plan — what happens in what order, what each phase must produce, and what kills the project at each gate. The working expectation is 3–4× useful inference per watt versus Jetson Thor (2.9× at first silicon, 4.2× with the mature compiler); the frozen minimum bar is 2×, the design target 3×, and the all-adverse floor 1.9× — the simulation page carries the numbers.

How the plan is run

  • Staged spend, kill tests first. Every phase ends in a gate that can end the project cheaply. Silicon money moves only after every pre-silicon gate passes.
  • Design partners before tapeout. Two to three frontier robotics teams — humanoids, manipulation, warehouse, heavy machinery, surgical — providing real workloads and a conditional commitment: “hit this latency at these watts, support these operators, and we will integrate.”
  • Coprocessor first. The robot keeps its SoC for cameras, ROS, networking, safety, and control; the RPU takes the model. Customers try us without leaving NVIDIA or Qualcomm. The deliverable is a pipeline — PyTorch/JAX in, compiled RPU schedule out — with unsupported work partitioned back to the GPU or CPU.
  • Generality is a tested property. Every gate is scored across model families — a DreamZero-class world-action model, a π₀/GR00T-class VLA, a latent/recurrent world model — not just the flagship workload.

Already banked

The plan starts from evidence, not a blank page: a performance simulator calibrated to measured Blackwell silicon (0.5–3.9 percent latency, 0.8–2.5 percent energy); an independent cycle-level model agreeing to 0.7 percent with no shared code; measured negative results (frequency scaling buys nothing; FP8 delivered 1.77×, not the marketed 2×); the comparison corrected to Thor’s dense-compute rating; and a sealed, falsifiable prediction for the first cross-architecture benchmark, registered before touching the hardware.

Phase 0 — now to 6 months

  • Recruit the two hires that gate everything downstream: an exceptional ASIC/accelerator architect and a compiler/performance architect.
  • Customer discovery toward the 2–3 design partners, anchored on the qualifying statement: “the larger model is much better, but Thor cannot run it onboard at the frequency and power we need.”
  • Measure: run the sealed Orin benchmark against its registered prediction, then real Thor hardware at 40 W on frozen workloads — replacing estimates with measurements on both sides of every published ratio.
  • Physical tile: synthesize the exact arithmetic tile and add measured SRAM, network-on-chip, and clock energy.
  • FPGA v0: the scaled RPU on reprogrammable hardware.

Gate: the mapped advantage from the physical tile must clear 2.2× — a line set just above the success bar, so the kill test and the success test nearly coincide. Below it, we stop.

Phase 1 — 6 to 12 months

  • Validate the memory system and compiler on the FPGA: SRAM behavior, NoC, DMA scheduling, and predicted-versus-real activity counts.
  • Multi-model sweep: all three model families across model size, token count, context length, planning depth, and software efficiency.
  • RPU v1 architecture frozen, with partner workloads compiled end to end and latency/power predicted before deployment.

Gate (~12 months): the pre-tapeout go/no-go. Proceed only if physically grounded modeling still predicts a compelling advantage across plausible future model architectures — not just the one we started from — and the design partners’ conditional commitments still stand against the measured numbers.

Phase 2 — to roughly 2 years

Tapeout, with a strong team and funding raised against the pre-silicon evidence. The compiler keeps maturing against the FPGA and partner models while the silicon is in flight — software maturity is where most of the multiplier growth lives, and it needs no physics to break our way.

Phase 3 — roughly 3 years

A credible first-generation product at the 2.9×-class design point: bring-up, the first partner integrations as coprocessor modules, and the profiling data that drives the compiler from its launch extraction toward maturity.

Phase 4 — 3 to 4 years

The mature compiler plus second-generation silicon, toward the 4×-class point — and, if the demand question below keeps resolving in favor of onboard compute, the platform position: the hardware abstraction and optimization layer for physical-AI inference.

The two standing questions every gate watches

Demand. Does additional onboard inference compute keep translating into robot capability? If frontier models shrink until Thor has abundant headroom, the opportunity weakens substantially. Current trajectory — robot-pretraining scaling laws, growing context, test-time compute arriving in robotics — points the other way, but this is re-asked at every gate, not assumed.

Supply. Can the RPU deliver its 3–4×? The instrument says 2.9–4.2× with a 1.9× all-adverse floor; if physical validation drags the real answer toward ~1.3×, integration risk overwhelms the benefit and the go/no-go says no.
