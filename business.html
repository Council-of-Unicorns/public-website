The plan

Substrate builds the RPU — the Robot Processing Unit: a companion chip and its compiler that let a robot run a far more capable AI model on the same 20–40 watts than today’s general-purpose edge GPUs. The pitch explains why this company should exist and the whitepaper explains how the chip works. This page is the plan: what we do in what order, what each stage must produce, and what would make us stop.

The numbers underneath the plan: we currently expect 3–4× more useful AI inference per watt than NVIDIA’s Jetson Thor, the best edge GPU for robots — 2.9× with our first chip, 4.2× as our compiler matures. Before exploring any designs we froze a minimum bar of 2× and a design target of 3×. If every uncertainty breaks against us, our model reads 1.9×. The simulation page shows where each number comes from.

How we work

We spend in stages, and every stage ends with a test that can end the project cheaply. We do not spend tapeout money until the preceding gates have passed. Customers are one of those tests, not a hope: before we commit a design to manufacturing, we require two or three signed letters of intent from design partners — frontier robotics teams in humanoids, manipulation, warehouse automation, heavy machinery, or surgical robotics — built on real workloads and plain terms: hit this speed at this power, support these models, and we will integrate it.

We ship a companion chip first, not a replacement for the robot’s main computer. The robot keeps its existing processor for cameras, networking, safety, and conventional control; the RPU runs the AI model. So a customer can try us without walking away from their NVIDIA or Qualcomm investment: their model goes into our compiler, comes out running on the RPU, and anything we do not yet support keeps running where it already runs.

And we do not bet on one model architecture. Robot AI is young — the architecture the industry finally settles on probably does not exist yet, and a chip hardwired to today’s would be obsolete before it shipped. What we are building is the machinery that makes co-design cheap: a calibrated simulator and compiler that can take any new model and tell us, within days, how fast it runs on any candidate chip. Every stage of this plan is tested against three different model families, and the same machinery will shape the silicon around whichever architecture wins. The chip is a product of that flywheel — the tooling we use to design every generation, not just the first.

First-year equipment is unglamorous: Thor devkits, FPGA boards, cloud GPUs, and a bench that measures power properly. The gates are an evidence order, not a calendar: when the early gates pass decisively, the test shuttle overlaps the production work rather than queueing in front of it — that is the compression the timeline below assumes.

How we design the chip

Our design process follows the five-step algorithm Elon Musk drilled into SpaceX and Tesla (his own description here), in order — because chip design punishes exactly the failure mode the ordering exists to prevent: optimizing, or automating, a thing that should not exist.

  1. Make the requirements less dumb. Challenge every assumption, starting with the biggest one we inherited: that real-time robot control can depend on remote inference. We built those systems at frontier robotics companies — datacenter GPUs thousands of miles from the robot, held together by heroic streaming infrastructure (the whitepaper tells that story) — and the engineering was excellent while the requirement was wrong. A robot’s control loop should not include a datacenter. Everything else in this plan follows from deleting that dependency.
  2. Delete the part. Most of a GPU’s area and energy exists so it can run anything. A robot runs one thing. The RPU executes the model with zero instructions fetched and zero kernels launched — the schedule is wired into the silicon, and the machinery of generality is simply gone.
  3. Simplify and optimize — only what survives deletion: one dataflow, memory managed by the compiler instead of caches guessing, and optimization spent where the joules actually go.
  4. Accelerate cycle time. This is the flywheel above: a new model or a new candidate design gets scored in days by simulation, and proven on reprogrammable hardware before any silicon is manufactured.
  5. Automate. The compiler is the automation — and so is our evidence: every number on this site is generated by the instrument, and the pages check themselves against it.

The next six months

The first three months are about people: recruit the founding team — an exceptional chip architect and a compiler architect, as cofounders — and bring on advisors who have built in this industry, in both robotics and silicon. Those people gate everything downstream. Customer discovery runs in parallel — we are already in discussions with executives at frontier robotics companies — finding the teams for whom this sentence is already true, “the larger model is much better, but Thor cannot run it onboard at the speed and power we need,” and turning those conversations into the signed letters of intent the plan requires.

We already have a performance simulator that reproduces our lab measurements of NVIDIA’s latest silicon to within a few percent on both speed and energy — with a second simulator, built independently with no shared code, agreeing to better than one percent. Early measurements have already changed our assumptions: slowing the clock saves no energy, halving precision delivered 1.77× where marketing says 2×, and NVIDIA’s headline number assumed a feature our workload cannot use, so the honest comparison is better for us than the marketing one. When we measure, we write down the predicted result first. The next six months extend that record to real hardware:

  • Buy Jetson Thor hardware and measure it directly at 40 watts on our frozen workloads, with our predictions written down before the first run — replacing every estimate about the incumbent with a measurement.
  • Build the chip’s core compute block with real silicon-design tools and measure its energy directly, then add the memory and data-movement costs the same way — numbers from tools, not from a spreadsheet.
  • Get a scaled-down RPU running on reprogrammable hardware (an FPGA).

If the measured compute block cannot clear 2.2× — the level below which our 2× bar can no longer be met with confidence in every operating mode, well under the 2.9× we expect — that is the end of it: a finding that costs weeks and a software license, not a fab run.

Six to twelve months

Prove the parts a spreadsheet cannot: that the chip’s memory system and data movement behave the way the compiler predicts, demonstrated on the FPGA. Sweep all three model families across model size, context length, and software quality. Then freeze a first chip design — chosen by the machinery against the models of that moment — with partner workloads compiling end to end and speed and power predicted before anything ships.

Around the one-year mark we make the biggest call: commit the design to manufacturing, or walk away. By then nothing about that decision is hypothetical — the advantage is measured rather than modeled, it has been tested across the model families that plausibly come next, and our design partners have seen the numbers.

The second year

If those gates pass, year two is tapeout: we commit the design to manufacturing early in the year, raise the silicon round against the pre-silicon evidence, and build the team to carry the design through physical implementation and bring-up. While the chip is being fabricated the compiler keeps improving against the FPGA and partner models, where most of the multiplier’s growth lives. By the end of the year: first chips running, first partner integrations as companion modules, and the real-world profiling data that pushes the compiler toward maturity — a first-generation RPU around the 2.9× design point, shipped to the initial partners.

Year two onwards: the real bet

The moment the first chip is in production, the company’s center of gravity shifts entirely to the flywheel: the process and tooling for co-designing the next chip together with the model architecture the industry converges on.

That is the real long-term bet. Three curves are heading toward the same intersection: model architectures converging, model compression maturing, and silicon advances — stacked memory above all — arriving on schedule. A chip designed at that intersection, for a known architecture instead of a moving target, is not a 2× story. Our instrument’s honest bound for it is an order of magnitude over a Jetson-class part: 15.7× with every input at its favorable end, 22× if gated low-voltage circuit techniques prove out — the simulation page publishes the range and labels it a bound, not a promise — which means running the largest world-action models of the future in real time, onboard, much, much faster than is possible on NVIDIA’s general-purpose chips. That bound is our north star — not underwritten, pursued. Beyond it sits the optimized RPU, already bounded: the chip rebuilt around 3D DRAM, the whole model living in memory stacked directly on the compute die, worth roughly 20–44× at most if everything proves. And past that, codesign: the model and the silicon designed together — a planning band of roughly 7–19×, an estimate, capped only by the physics of the arithmetic itself. The second-generation chip is the first product of that flywheel, and the deployment layer it creates is what the company ultimately sells.

The two questions we keep asking

Does more onboard compute still buy more capable robots? If frontier models shrink until Thor has headroom to spare, this opportunity weakens substantially. Our current bet is that demand for onboard compute keeps increasing: scaling laws emerging in robot pretraining, growing context windows, and test-time reasoning arriving in robotics. But we re-test that assumption at every stage.

Can the RPU really deliver 3–4×? Our instrument says 2.9–4.2× today, with a floor of 1.9× if everything breaks against us. If physical measurement drags the real answer down toward 1.3×, the cost of integrating a new chip outweighs the benefit, and the one-year decision says stop. That is exactly what it is for.
