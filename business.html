Business

The plan

Substrate builds the RPU — the Robot Processing Unit: an inference coprocessor and compiler stack that let a robot run a far more capable model on the same 20–40 watts than a general-purpose edge GPU can. The pitch explains why this company should exist and the whitepaper explains how the chip works. This page is the plan: what we do in what order, what each stage has to produce, and what would make us stop.

The numbers the plan is built on: we currently expect 3–4× more useful inference per watt than Jetson Thor — 2.9× at first silicon, 4.2× once the compiler matures. The bar we froze before exploring the design space is 2×, the design target is 3×, and if every uncertain input goes against us the model reads 1.9×. The simulation page carries all of it.

How we work

We spend in stages, and every stage ends with a test that can end the project cheaply. Money for silicon moves only after everything before it has passed. Before tapeout we want two or three design partners — frontier robotics teams in humanoids, manipulation, warehouse automation, heavy machinery, or surgical robotics — who give us real workloads and tell us plainly: hit this latency at these watts, support these operators, and we will integrate it.

We ship a coprocessor first, not a Jetson replacement. The robot keeps its existing SoC for cameras, ROS, networking, safety, and control; the RPU takes the model. That means a customer can try us without walking away from their NVIDIA or Qualcomm investment. The experience we owe them: a PyTorch or JAX model goes in, a compiled RPU schedule comes out, and anything we do not support keeps running where it already runs.

And we do not commit to a model architecture. World-action models are young; the architecture the industry converges on probably does not exist yet, and a chip hardwired to today’s would be obsolete before it shipped. What we are building instead is the flywheel: a calibrated simulator, an independent cycle model, an energy ledger, and a compiler that together turn any new model into a scored design point in days. Every checkpoint is tested across model families — a DreamZero-class world-action model, a π₀/GR00T-class vision-language-action model, a latent world model — and the same machinery will co-design the silicon with whatever architecture wins. The chip is a product of the flywheel; the flywheel is the company.

Where we are today

This plan does not start from a blank page. We have a performance simulator calibrated against measured Blackwell silicon to within 0.5–3.9 percent on latency and 0.8–2.5 percent on energy, and an independent cycle-level model that agrees with it to 0.7 percent while sharing no code. We have published our own negative results: slowing the clock saves nothing on modern GPUs, and halving precision delivered 1.77× where marketing says 2×. We corrected our comparison to Thor’s dense-compute rating — the only one our workload is allowed to use. And the next decisive measurement is already sealed: we registered a prediction for the Jetson Orin benchmark before touching the hardware, so our first cross-architecture test can prove us wrong.

The next six months

The first three months are about people: recruit the founding team — an exceptional ASIC/accelerator architect and a compiler/performance architect, as cofounders — and bring on advisors who have actually built in this industry, both robotics and silicon. Those people gate everything downstream. Customer discovery runs in parallel, looking for the teams for whom this sentence is already true: “the larger model is much better, but Thor cannot run it onboard at the frequency and power we need.”

The rest of the six months is measurement and first hardware evidence:

  • Run the sealed Orin benchmark against its registered prediction, then put real Thor hardware on the bench at 40 watts with frozen workloads — so both sides of every published ratio are measured, not estimated.
  • Physically synthesize the exact arithmetic tile and add measured SRAM, network-on-chip, and clock energy.
  • Get a scaled RPU running on an FPGA.

We stop here unless the synthesized tile shows at least a 2.2× advantage — a line we set just above the success bar, so the test that could kill the project and the test that proves it are nearly the same test.

Six to twelve months

Validate the parts a spreadsheet cannot: SRAM behavior, the network-on-chip, DMA scheduling, and whether the compiler’s predicted activity matches what the FPGA actually does. Sweep all three model families across model size, tokens, context length, planning depth, and software quality. Then freeze an RPU v1 architecture — chosen by that machinery against the model families of the moment, not by attachment to any one of them — with partner workloads compiling end to end and latency and power predicted before anything is deployed.

At roughly the one-year mark comes the decision that matters most: go or no-go on tapeout. We proceed only if physically grounded modeling still shows a compelling advantage across the model architectures that plausibly come next — not just the one we started from — and our design partners’ commitments still stand against measured numbers.

The second year

Tapeout, with a team and funding raised on the pre-silicon evidence. While the silicon is in flight, the compiler keeps maturing against the FPGA and partner models — most of the multiplier’s growth lives in software, and that work needs no physics to break our way.

Year three

A credible first-generation product at the 2.9×-class design point: bring-up, the first partner integrations as coprocessor modules, and the profiling data that pushes the compiler from its launch quality toward maturity.

Years three to four

The mature compiler and second-generation silicon, toward the 4×-class point. If demand for onboard compute keeps growing the way it has, this is where the platform ambition becomes real: the optimization layer that physical-AI models get deployed through.

The two questions we keep asking

Does more onboard compute still buy more capable robots? If frontier models shrink until Thor has headroom to spare, this opportunity weakens substantially. Everything in the current trajectory — scaling laws emerging in robot pretraining, growing context windows, test-time compute arriving in robotics — points the other way. But we re-ask this at every stage instead of assuming it.

Can the RPU really deliver 3–4×? The instrument says 2.9–4.2× today, with a floor of 1.9× if everything breaks against us. If physical validation drags the real answer down toward 1.3×, the integration cost outweighs the benefit and the one-year decision says stop. That is exactly what it is there for.
