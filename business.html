Business

A compute platform for
frontier physical AI

Substrate builds hardware and software that lets robots spend dramatically more inference compute on perception, world modeling, memory, and planning within the same onboard power envelope — so frontier models that are impractical on general-purpose edge GPUs become deployable. The strongest version of this company is not an ASIC for one model. It is a physical-AI inference platform designed around the constraints of intelligent robots that do not change when the models do.

The thesis

The core bet is not that any particular model needs acceleration. It is that frontier robot intelligence will remain inference-compute hungry — even as that compute shifts between larger models, more visual tokens, longer temporal context, world modeling, planning, richer modalities, or test-time reasoning. Wherever the compute goes, it stays dense, low-precision, latency-bound, and onboard. A robot’s head sheds roughly 40 watts of heat; that ceiling is set by physics and packaging, not by any model generation, and it is the constraint we design against.

The product

We start with the RPU: an inference coprocessor module, not a full Jetson replacement. The robot keeps its existing SoC for cameras and ISP, ROS, networking, safety, and conventional control. The RPU takes what the SoC cannot handle: vision-language-action and world-model inference, large dense tensor workloads, persistent world state and KV memory, low-latency batch-1 execution, and future model families through a programmable compiler stack.

The customer experience is a pipeline, not a porting project: a PyTorch or JAX model goes into our optimization and compiler stack and comes out running on the RPU, with minimal model rewriting. Work the RPU does not support is partitioned back onto the GPU or CPU rather than blocking the deployment.

Who it is for

Frontier robotics teams whose best model works but cannot be deployed onboard at the latency and power they need. The customer statement we are built for:

“The larger, richer model is much better — but Thor cannot run it onboard at the frequency and power we need.”

The initial segments where that sentence is already true: humanoids, general-purpose manipulation, advanced warehouse robotics, autonomous heavy machinery, and surgical and medical robotics — high-value robots where model capability matters more than a few hundred dollars of bill-of-materials.

What we sell

Not “more efficient silicon.” We sell the ability to run a materially more capable robot model inside the same 20–40 W envelope. An efficiency multiple is fungible: the customer can spend it as a larger model, more cameras, longer memory, more planning steps, lower latency, or lower power — whichever moves their product.

The numbers behind that promise, from our calibrated instrument, scored at 40 W power parity against Jetson Thor’s honest dense-compute rating: the current design points read 2.9× at first silicon, rising to 4.2× as the compiler matures — so the working expectation across the product’s early life is 3–4× useful inference per watt. The minimum technical bar is 2×, frozen in a machine-readable contract before we explored the design space, and the design target is 3×. We publish the honest downside too: with every uncertain input at its adverse end the model reads 1.9×, just below the bar — which is why the plan below is a sequence of kill tests rather than a road show.

Why the architecture can win

We deliberately avoid hardwiring one transient model architecture. The specialization is around characteristics of physical-AI inference that have been stable across every model family we have measured: batch-1 execution, strict latency, dense low-precision tensor operations, deterministic and static schedules, large sequential weight and state movement, high temporal locality, persistent latent and KV state, operator fusion, action and world-model pipelines, and multiple inference timescales running together. One identity does a lot of quiet work here: at frontier scale the weights never fit on-die — a 14-billion-parameter model at 4-bit is about 7 GB against tens of megabytes of SRAM — so every architecture in this class streams weights the same way, and specializing for the stream generalizes across models nearly for free.

The candidate architectural advantages: deterministic tensor streaming in the spirit of Groq, compiler-managed SRAM in place of conventional cache behavior, a specialized SRAM hierarchy, sharply reduced network-on-chip and control overhead, resident package memory, eventually hybrid-bonded 3D memory, and possibly robotics-specific compute such as temporal-differential processing. None of these count until physically validated — our own energy ledger prices each one, and its coefficients are labeled estimates until a physical design kit or measurement pins them.

The software moat

The compiler is the product as much as the silicon. It profiles customer models, fuses operators, chooses tiling and layout, schedules DMA, manages SRAM and package memory, routes tensors, partitions unsupported work onto the GPU or CPU, and predicts latency and power before deployment. We can afford a first-class compiler because the target is narrow: one class of static-graph workload compiled extremely well, not CUDA breadth.

Software maturity is also where most of the multiplier growth lives, and we track it as four measurable quantities — array utilization, fusion quality, DMA overlap, and idle gating — rather than as a vibe. The entire gap between the 2.9× first-silicon point and the 4.2× mature point is the compiler axis: it is in-house, schedule-driven, and needs no physics to break our way. Longer term, the stack can become a hardware abstraction and optimization layer for physical AI, making model portability and pre-deployment performance prediction part of what customers buy.

Go-to-market

A small number of design partners rather than broad developer adoption. The goal before tapeout is two to three serious robotics design partners who provide real workloads and commit to the shape of a deal: “if you hit this latency at these watts and support these operators, we will evaluate and integrate it.” Selling initially as a coprocessor means customers do not abandon their NVIDIA or Qualcomm infrastructure to try us — the integration risk is a module, not a re-platform.

What is already validated

This plan does not start from a blank page. We have a performance simulator calibrated against measured Blackwell silicon to within 0.5–3.9 percent on latency and 0.8–2.5 percent on energy; an independent cycle-level model that agrees with it to 0.7 percent while sharing no code; and published negative results from our own bench — frequency scaling buys nothing on modern GPUs, and halving precision measured 1.77× rather than the marketed 2×. The comparison charges Thor its honest dense-compute peak, corrected against NVIDIA’s own product tables. And the next decisive measurement is already sealed: a prediction for the Jetson Orin benchmark was registered before touching the hardware, so the instrument’s first cross-architecture test is falsifiable, not curve-fit.

Validation roadmap

  1. Recruit. An exceptional ASIC/accelerator architect and a compiler/performance architect. These two hires gate everything downstream.
  2. Measure. Run the sealed Orin benchmark against its registered prediction, then real Thor hardware at 40 W on frozen workloads — replacing our Thor estimates with measurements on both sides of every published ratio.
  3. Build pre-silicon evidence. Physically synthesize the exact arithmetic tile and add measured SRAM, network-on-chip, and clock energy — the kill line is a mapped advantage below 2.2×, sitting just above the success bar. Build a scaled FPGA RPU, and validate the compiler and scheduler against real activity counts.
  4. Validate across model families. A DreamZero-like world-action model, a π₀/GR00T-class vision-language-action model, and a latent/recurrent world model, swept across model size, token count, context length, planning depth, and software efficiency. Our simulator already scores a JEPA-style family through the identical accounting as the flagship — generality is a tested property, not a slide.
  5. Go/no-go. Proceed toward tapeout only if physically grounded modeling still predicts a compelling advantage across plausible future model architectures — not just the one we started from.

The two questions that could kill it

Demand. Does additional onboard inference compute keep translating into meaningful robot capability? If frontier models become small enough that Thor has abundant headroom, the opportunity weakens substantially. Everything in the industry’s current trajectory — scaling laws emerging in robot pretraining, context windows growing, test-time compute arriving in robotics — points the other way, but we treat this as a live question and revisit it at every gate.

Supply. Can the RPU actually deliver 2–3× or more useful inference per watt than an optimized Thor? The instrument currently says 2.9–4.2× with an all-adverse floor of 1.9×; if physical validation drags the real answer toward ~1.3×, integration risk overwhelms the benefit and we stop before tapeout money. That is precisely what the kill tests are for.

Timeline

  • 0–6 months: recruit the two architects, customer discovery, Thor and Orin measurements, the synthesized arithmetic tile, FPGA v0.
  • 6–12 months: SRAM/NoC/compiler validation, the multi-model simulator sweep, a credible RPU v1 architecture.
  • ~12 months: the major pre-tapeout go/no-go decision.
  • ~2 years: tapeout; approach first silicon with a strong team and funding.
  • ~3 years: a credible first-generation product at the 2.9×-class design point.
  • 3–4 years: mature compiler plus second-generation silicon, toward the 4×-class point.
