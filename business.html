The plan

Substrate builds the RPU — the Robot Processing Unit: a companion chip and its compiler that let a robot run a far more capable AI model on the same 20–40 watts than today’s general-purpose edge GPUs. The pitch explains why this company should exist and the whitepaper explains how the chip works. This page is the plan: what we do in what order, what each stage must produce, and what would make us stop.

The numbers underneath the plan: we currently expect 3–4× more useful AI inference per watt than NVIDIA’s Jetson Thor, the best edge GPU for robots — 2.9× with our first chip, 4.2× as our compiler matures. Before exploring any designs we froze a minimum bar of 2× and a design target of 3×. If every uncertainty breaks against us, our model reads 1.9×. The simulation page shows where each number comes from.

How we work

We spend in stages, and every stage ends with a test that can end the project cheaply. Money for silicon moves only after everything before it has passed. Before we commit a design to manufacturing, we want two or three design partners — frontier robotics teams in humanoids, manipulation, warehouse automation, heavy machinery, or surgical robotics — who give us real workloads and tell us plainly: hit this speed at this power, support these models, and we will integrate it.

We ship a companion chip first, not a replacement for the robot’s main computer. The robot keeps its existing processor for cameras, networking, safety, and conventional control; the RPU runs the AI model. So a customer can try us without walking away from their NVIDIA or Qualcomm investment: their model goes into our compiler, comes out running on the RPU, and anything we do not yet support keeps running where it already runs.

And we do not bet on one model architecture. Robot AI is young — the architecture the industry finally settles on probably does not exist yet, and a chip hardwired to today’s would be obsolete before it shipped. What we are building is the machinery that makes co-design cheap: a calibrated simulator and compiler that can take any new model and tell us, within days, how fast it runs on any candidate chip. Every stage of this plan is tested against three different model families, and the same machinery will shape the silicon around whichever architecture wins. The chip is a product of that flywheel; the flywheel is the company.

How we design the chip

Our design process follows the five-step algorithm Elon Musk drilled into SpaceX and Tesla (his own description here), in order — because chip design punishes exactly the failure mode the ordering exists to prevent: optimizing, or automating, a thing that should not exist.

  1. Make the requirements less dumb. Challenge every assumption, including ours. That habit is how we caught NVIDIA’s headline rating assuming a feature our workload cannot use, why we bench-tested the claim that halving precision doubles speed (it delivered 1.77×), and why peak operations per second appears nowhere in our requirements — at 40 watts, energy per operation is the only number that matters.
  2. Delete the part. Most of a GPU’s area and energy exists so it can run anything. A robot runs one thing. The RPU executes the model with zero instructions fetched and zero kernels launched — the schedule is wired into the silicon, and the machinery of generality is simply gone.
  3. Simplify and optimize — only what survives deletion: one dataflow, memory managed by the compiler instead of caches guessing, and optimization spent where the joules actually go.
  4. Accelerate cycle time. This is the flywheel above: a new model or a new candidate design gets scored in days by simulation, and proven on reprogrammable hardware before any silicon is manufactured.
  5. Automate. The compiler is the automation — and so is our evidence: every number on this site is generated by the instrument, and the pages check themselves against it.

Where we are today

This plan does not start from a blank page. We built a performance simulator and required it to reproduce real hardware before trusting it: it matches our lab measurements of NVIDIA’s latest silicon to within a few percent on both speed and energy, and a second simulator, built independently with no shared code, agrees with it to better than one percent. We publish our own negative results — slowing the clock saves no energy, and halving precision delivered 1.77× where marketing says 2× — and when we found NVIDIA’s headline number assumed a feature our workload cannot use, we corrected the comparison and our advantage went up. When we measure, we write down the predicted result first, so every test can prove us wrong.

The next six months

The first three months are about people: recruit the founding team — an exceptional chip architect and a compiler architect, as cofounders — and bring on advisors who have built in this industry, in both robotics and silicon. Those people gate everything downstream. Customer discovery runs in parallel, looking for the teams for whom this sentence is already true: “the larger model is much better, but Thor cannot run it onboard at the speed and power we need.”

The rest of the six months is measurement and first hardware evidence:

  • Buy Jetson Thor hardware and measure it directly at 40 watts on our frozen workloads, with our predictions written down before the first run — replacing every estimate about the incumbent with a measurement.
  • Build the chip’s core compute block with real silicon-design tools and measure its energy directly, then add the memory and data-movement costs the same way — numbers from tools, not from a spreadsheet.
  • Get a scaled-down RPU running on reprogrammable hardware (an FPGA).

If the measured compute block cannot clear a 2.2× advantage, that is the end of it — a finding that costs weeks and a software license, not a fab run.

Six to twelve months

Prove the parts a spreadsheet cannot: that the chip’s memory system and data movement behave the way the compiler predicts, demonstrated on the FPGA. Sweep all three model families across model size, context length, and software quality. Then freeze a first chip design — chosen by the machinery against the models of that moment, not by attachment to any one of them — with partner workloads compiling end to end and speed and power predicted before anything ships.

Around the one-year mark we make the biggest call: commit the design to manufacturing, or walk away. By then nothing about that decision is hypothetical — the advantage is measured rather than modeled, it has been tested across the model families that plausibly come next, and our design partners have seen the numbers.

The second year

Tapeout — committing the design to manufacturing — with a team and funding raised on the pre-silicon evidence. While the chip is being fabricated, the compiler keeps improving against the FPGA and partner models. Most of the multiplier’s growth lives in software, and that work needs no physics to break our way.

Year three

A credible first product at the 2.9×-class design point: first chips running, first partner integrations as companion modules, and the real-world profiling data that pushes the compiler toward maturity.

Years three to four

The mature compiler and a second-generation chip, toward the 4×-class point. If demand for onboard AI keeps growing the way it has, this is where the platform ambition becomes real: the layer that physical-AI models get deployed through.

The two questions we keep asking

Does more onboard compute still buy more capable robots? If frontier models shrink until Thor has headroom to spare, this opportunity weakens substantially. Everything in the current trajectory — scaling laws emerging in robot pretraining, growing context windows, test-time reasoning arriving in robotics — points the other way. But we re-ask this at every stage instead of assuming it.

Can the RPU really deliver 3–4×? Our instrument says 2.9–4.2× today, with a floor of 1.9× if everything breaks against us. If physical measurement drags the real answer down toward 1.3×, the cost of integrating a new chip outweighs the benefit, and the one-year decision says stop. That is exactly what it is for.
