The chip

Etching a world model into silicon

A 14-billion-parameter world model has to produce an action every 200 ms inside a 40 W robot head. This page walks through the phase-1 concept: what is on the die, how it runs the transformer with no instructions at all, and how much faster that makes it than a Jetson Thor. Every number comes from the simulator calibrated against measured Blackwell silicon. The die drawings are illustrative; the numbers are not.

The die

Where the die area goes

A GPU spends most of its area and energy on being able to run anything — schedulers, instruction caches, register files, coherent caches. None of that is free, and a fixed policy never uses any of it. We hardwire one dataflow and spend what that frees on FP4 multiply-accumulate. Hover any block to see what it costs.

The etch

One chunk, start to finish

Here is one 200 ms control chunk, start to finish. Weights stream out of DRAM once per diffusion step, and both guidance branches read the same stream, so the second branch is free. The two-second context window is read each step and shifts in place at the end of the chunk. Nothing here launches a kernel or fetches an instruction: the sequence below is wired into the silicon, which is also where the deadline guarantee comes from. Stage lengths are the model’s real timings, slowed about 40× so you can watch them.

The payoff

How much faster, and what it depends on

Both chips run out of power before they run out of arithmetic, so what sets the speed is energy per FLOP, not peak FLOPS. The slider is η, how much less energy our datapath spends per FLOP than the Blackwell silicon we measured. Everything else is held fixed: same workload, same utilization model for both chips, and the uncertain inputs swept rather than guessed. Transformer ASICs have historically landed somewhere around η = 2–5.
