The instrument

Staying a multiple ahead of Jetson Thor

The question that matters is not whether today’s largest world model runs in a head today. Models are shrinking, silicon keeps improving, and that bar moves on its own. What we care about is staying a fixed multiple ahead of Jetson Thor while it moves. This page is the raw readout of the constraint everything else sits on, and it does not flatter us: our own row is scored with no architecture advantage at all. That is on purpose. It is the floor, not the forecast.

Why it lasts

The bar moves. The multiple should not.

Everything above is measured against a 14 B model, which is the largest thing anyone is currently trying to put in a head. That is deliberately the hardest case, and it is also a moving target — the published models have been getting smaller quickly.

So absolute feasibility arrives on its own eventually, for us and for NVIDIA alike. Which is why we do not price the company on it. What we price is the ratio, and there are three reasons it survives the bar moving.

Power efficiency improves far slower than headline performance. Huang’s law is quoted as 25× every five years, but that is throughput. Measured FLOP/s per watt doubles roughly every three to four years, and between 2014 and 2020 a ~6× gain in FLOPs came with ~2× the power draw. Inside a 30 W head you do not get to spend more power, so Thor’s successors inherit the slow curve rather than the fast one.

η is architecture, not process. Our advantage comes from deleting generality: no scheduler, no instruction fetch, no coherent cache, no kernel launches. A node shrink is available to both of us, so it moves both numbers and leaves the ratio where it was. That is what makes η an asset rather than a lead we have to keep re-winning.

NVIDIA cannot take it without giving up the product. CUDA generality is what they sell. Hameed and colleagues measured only 5.8 % of a general-purpose chip’s energy reaching the transistors that do arithmetic; a fixed datapath pushes that toward 35 %. Closing that gap means abandoning the generality their entire robotics stack depends on.

The honest version of the downside: if models shrink far enough that a general SoC clears the deadline in-head on its own, our wedge narrows from only we can do this to we do it faster and cooler. We would still run the same model on a fraction of the energy, or a considerably larger model in the same envelope — and on current trends the models are getting more capable, not just smaller.

Verdicts

Nobody clears the bar today, Thor included

On a 14 B model at head power, no row clears all three bars — Jetson Thor included. That is the useful finding. Absolute real-time on today’s largest model is not a bar anyone is standing over, so it cannot be the thing that separates us. The comparison that can is relative, and our row here is deliberately its own worst case: same utilization model as the incumbent, no etched-dataflow advantage credited, η = 1. It is the number we multiply, not the number we ship.

Latency

Where the 200 ms budget goes

Per-stage chunk time against the 200 ms deadline, marked by the vertical rule. Thor misses it by about 2.5×; our η = 1 floor misses by about 9×. Only the B200 fits, at datacenter power a thousand miles away, which is the problem this project exists to remove. Energy

Almost all of it is compute

At the measured calibration of about 0.33 pJ per FP4 FLOP, compute is ~96 % of every chunk’s energy and every row draws more than its own rated power — the B200 by more than three times. That is not a modelling artifact left over from placeholder constants; it survived calibration. It is what the energy-rate bound looks like when you hold real silicon to this workload. Design space

Compute moves the answer. Bandwidth does not.

Chunk latency across a grid of FP4 compute against memory bandwidth (darker is faster). The green cells form vertical stripes, which tells you where to spend: compute moves the answer and bandwidth barely does. At one distilled step, anything past 2 PF clears at every bandwidth on the chart, Thor’s 273 GB/s included — so LPDDR is enough and HBM is not worth its power. No cell clears 30 W by brute force, which is precisely why the advantage has to come from energy per FLOP rather than from more silicon.

Sensitivity

Step compression and token count carry the conclusion

How far each uncertain input moves the answer on its own, with the others held fixed. The two knobs that shrink the workload — how many denoising steps, and how many tokens — dominate everything else. Realized bandwidth utilization barely registers, which is exactly what you would want to see if the loop really is compute-bound. It is a useful sign the model is not fooling itself.

What it costs to keep the chip general enough to run a second family of models. The union point — half again as much FP4 array and a 35 W budget, sized to cover a JEPA-MPC workload — against the DreamZero-specialized point, both running the identical DreamZero path.
