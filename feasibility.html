Same power as the best edge GPU. How much faster is a chip built for one job?

We compare our chip and NVIDIA's Jetson Thor at identical power, 40 watts, the most a robot's head can cool. At matched power the physics is clean: the speed ratio equals the efficiency ratio, exactly. Beating Thor twice over means using half the energy per operation. Purpose-built chips have delivered gains in that range before, and this page shows the test, the result, and what would prove us wrong.

How we tested it

Before designing any silicon, we built a performance simulator and required it to reproduce real hardware before trusting anything it says about hardware that does not exist yet. We ran the world-model workload on an NVIDIA Blackwell workstation GPU in our lab, measured latency and power at the wall, and tuned the simulator until it matched. It now reproduces our measurements within 0.7 to 4.6 percent on latency and within 0.2 to 2.4 percent on energy, across four different configurations.

Every comparison below uses the same rules for both chips: identical power, identical utilization assumptions. Our design never gets a friendlier assumption than NVIDIA's.

The physics of the problem

A robot's head can shed roughly 40 watts of heat through the neck. Under that limit, the deciding factor is not how many operations per second a chip can advertise. A job that costs 100 joules takes at least 100 joules divided by 40 watts, two and a half seconds, no matter how fast the chip claims to be. Both chips hit this wall in our simulations, and it makes the comparison exact: with the same watts and the same job, speed is energy efficiency. We call the ratio the efficiency advantage.

The result

With no efficiency advantage the two chips tie: same watts, same energy per operation, same speed. From there, every gain in efficiency is a gain in speed, one for one. In its highest-quality planning mode, at our design target, the chip finishes a two-second imagined trajectory in 4.5 seconds where Thor needs 13.

We hold ourselves to the strict version of the goal: at least twice Thor's speed in every operating mode, from the highest-quality three-step planner down to the fastest one-step controller, judged at the pessimistic end of our simulation's uncertainty, not the average. That requires a 2.15× efficiency advantage. Our design target is 3×, which keeps the win intact even if our assumptions about Thor prove generous to us.

Why 2 to 3× is credible

Purpose-built AI chips have done this before. Google's TPUv4 delivered 1.6 to 3.2 times the performance per watt of NVIDIA's A100, measured on real deployments and published in a peer-reviewed paper. Our required 2.15 sits in the middle of that range and our 3× target at its top. On top of the architecture sit gains we deliberately do not count in the headline: the utilization advantage fixed-schedule chips hold under strict deadlines (Google measured its TPU sustaining 80 percent of peak under a latency limit where the GPU fell to 37 percent), workload tricks our chip runs better than GPUs do, and low-voltage circuit techniques one well-funded competitor reports working in silicon. Those are margin, not promises.

What we have already ruled out

We tested the two shortcuts everyone suggests, on our own hardware, and both failed. Slowing the clock to save energy does nothing on modern GPUs; we measured energy per operation flat across the entire frequency range. And halving numeric precision, which marketing materials say doubles efficiency, measured only 1.77 times. We publish these negative results because a plan built on numbers that survive testing is worth more than one built on numbers that sound good.

One important distinction

Beating Thor in every mode is a separate question from hitting the model's full five-updates-per-second target in its highest-quality mode. The first is a chip question and the answer is above. The second also depends on shrinking the model itself, work our team has done before at a frontier robotics company, and it is part of the plan rather than a promise we make on silicon alone.

What would prove us wrong

We spend money in stages, and each stage can kill the project cheaply. First, a detailed energy model of the core circuit block, built with open tools in weeks, for almost nothing. If it shows less than a 2.2× advantage, we stop; that kill line sits exactly at our success bar. Second, a few thousand dollars of Jetson Thor hardware to replace our Thor estimates with measurements. Third, the design running on reprogrammable hardware, which converts the simulated advantage into a measured one before any chip is manufactured. We only pay for silicon after all three pass.
