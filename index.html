Humanoid control is converging on world models: a fourteen‑billion‑parameter diffusion transformer that imagines the next two seconds, acts, and re‑plans five times a second. Today that takes a datacenter. We build the chip that runs it in the head.

01 — The deadline

A late answer is a fall,
not a dropped frame.

A two‑hundred‑millisecond hard deadline, inside a fanless head. Neither constraint gives.

Detail

The robot is already mid‑step when the answer comes due. Miss it and you do not get a stutter, you get a stability event.

That budget has to be met in a head whose neck path can reject roughly forty watts. This is not a throughput problem with a latency footnote. It is a deadline problem with a power ceiling, and the two constraints fight each other.

02 — What it costs today

I built the wrong
solution first.

Control rate took two B200s in a datacenter and a custom QUIC relay. That puts the radio inside the safety loop.

Detail

At 1X I was responsible for getting a fine‑tuned action co‑diffusion world model to control rate. It took the entire arsenal: sixteen‑to‑three‑step distillation, classifier‑free guidance split across two B200s, velocity caching between diffusion steps, graph capture to suppress kernel‑launch overhead, and train‑time chunking to fit the streaming regime. It worked — at two kilowatt‑class GPUs per robot.

So I built the other half of the obvious answer too, down to a custom media‑over‑QUIC layer streaming observations up and action chunks back. Building both is what made the shape of the mistake clear. No amount of transport engineering turns a wireless round trip into a certifiable miss rate. A humanoid that needs connectivity to keep its balance is a demo, not a product.

03 — The bet

Etch the dataflow.
Start with a systolic array.

Fixed graph, fixed shapes, weights resident, deterministic latency. The bet Google made with the TPU — aimed at the head.

Detail

The first chip is not exotic. It is a systolic array at the edge — the same bet Etched made for the datacenter and Groq made with the LPU, aimed at the one place none of them are looking. A fixed graph and fixed tensor shapes buy static scheduling, no kernel dispatch, and timing you can put in a safety case.

The generality is what costs you. A general‑purpose SoC spends die area and power on rasterization, display, and a CPU complex a fixed policy will never call. Reallocate that to tensor arrays and on‑chip SRAM and the same watts do considerably more useful work.

04 — Evidence

We built the instrument
before the chip.

Calibrated against measured Blackwell silicon to within 0.7–4.6 percent. It tells us FP8 buys 1.77×, not 2×.

Detail

Before claiming anything, we built a simulator that has to reproduce real silicon first. Every hardware configuration shares one utilization model, so the comparisons stay honest rather than flattering the part we want to build.

It also tells us things we would rather not hear. Voltage and frequency scaling buys essentially nothing. Hitting 5 Hz on the nose will need model‑side compression as well as silicon — that is a measurement, not an assumption. We would rather publish the ceiling than discover it in fab.
