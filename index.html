RPU — Robot Processing Unit

We build the chip around the robot’s world action model.

Real‑time robot‑policy inference, inside the robot, at 40 watts.

Detail

Frontier robot policies are becoming too large for general-purpose edge GPUs. A DreamZero-class model has roughly 14 billion parameters, imagines future trajectories, and must re-plan several times per second. Today, workloads at this scale still require datacenter-class compute. We are building the RPU: a companion accelerator and compiler designed to run them in the robot’s head.

01 — The requirement

Real time, on board,
in any environment.

The RPU has to run a 14-billion-parameter robot policy inside the robot’s head on roughly 40 watts, re-planning faster than a human’s ~200 ms reaction time — and hold that timing predictably, anywhere.

Detail

That is batch-one inference — one control request at a time, with no batch to hide latency behind — and the chip has to return an action within the deadline on every cycle, not on average. Because the graph and tensor shapes never change, the schedule can be wired into the silicon: no operating system, no kernel dispatch, no caches guessing at the access pattern.

And all of it has to fit a head that can shed only about 40 W. That power ceiling — not peak throughput or memory bandwidth — is what the chip is designed against, so the metric that matters is useful inference per watt.

02 — Remote inference cannot scale beyond demos

We built the wrong
solution first.

Control rate took two B200s in a datacenter and a custom QUIC relay. That puts the network inside the control loop.

Detail

We have built inference stacks at frontier humanoid robotics companies. Real-time control required aggressive optimizations: step distillation, classifier‑free guidance split across two B200s, velocity caching between diffusion steps, decoupled noise schedules, graph capture to suppress kernel‑launch overhead, and train‑time chunking to fit the streaming regime. It worked — at two kilowatt‑class GPUs per robot.

So we built the other half of the obvious answer too, down to a custom media‑over‑QUIC layer streaming observations up and action chunks back. But no amount of transport engineering makes a wireless round trip predictable enough to design against. Robotics cannot scale beyond the demo if every robot depends on a remote GPU cluster and perfect connectivity.

03 — The RPU

Trade generality
for useful compute.

A GPU must be able to run almost anything. A robot only needs to run one very specific thing — the same AI workload, over and over — at batch one and under a hard power budget.

Detail

The RPU exploits that difference. The compiler knows the graph, tensor shapes, operator order, and memory-access pattern ahead of time. The chip can statically schedule execution, stream weights predictably, keep intermediates local, and build its datapath around the operations that dominate the model.

The first RPU is a companion accelerator, not a replacement for the robot’s main computer. The existing SoC keeps cameras, networking, safety, and conventional control; the RPU runs the robot policy. Models and schedules stay loadable, so the chip can follow an architecture that is still changing.

04 — Why specialization wins at 40 W

At the edge,
energy efficiency is essential.

At a fixed power budget, going faster means spending fewer joules per inference. Most of a GPU’s energy is wasted on preserving generality.

Detail

The workload is compute-bound — about 16,000 FLOPs per byte against Thor’s ~3,800 roofline, roughly 4× on the compute side even giving Thor the best case [S][X]. So more bandwidth does not help; energy does. At 40 W the power floor dominates every other limit by an order of magnitude, and throughput is just P / E [S].

A specialized chip spends fewer joules per inference: a wired schedule instead of the ~1,200 GPU kernels a chunk launches, with local storage and deeper fusion. TPUv4 already delivered 1.6–3.2× higher performance per watt than an A100 at the same node [X]. We target 5× Thor at 40 W [T]; the simulator brackets first silicon at 2.7–6.7×, central 2.9× [S].

05 — The horizon

The first chip is the beginning
of the specialization curve.

Robot foundational models are still changing too quickly to etch one architecture into hardware. Our first product preserves enough programmability to adapt to evolving workloads while removing the generality tax on performance.

As the models stabilize, we specialize more aggressively: rebuild the memory system around the model, fix more of the datapath, harden stable operator chains, and eventually harden the stable weights themselves. The endpoint is not simply a faster processor running a robot policy. It is an ASIC whose architecture is the robot policy.
