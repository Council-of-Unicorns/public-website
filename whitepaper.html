Whitepaper — August 2026

A measured case for etching world-model inference into a robot’s head

The ChatGPT moment for robotics requires world models to run on the robot.

Source tags: [M] measured ourselves · [S] computed by our simulator · [F] measured in production at a robotics company · [X] published elsewhere · [T] a target, not yet reached.

Executive summary#

Humanoid robotics is converging on video-action diffusion transformers that predict several seconds of motion and must replan fast enough to achieve human-like reaction latency. Today’s leading models are still too large to run inside a robot head, forcing teams to rely on kilowatt-class datacenter GPUs and continuous network connections. That puts latency, Wi-Fi jitter, and connectivity failures directly inside the robot’s control loop [F].

Our immediate goal is to build a specialized accelerator that runs these workloads about 3× faster than NVIDIA Thor at the same 40-watt power budget [S]. That advantage creates an immediate competitive edge: more compelling robot demos, larger deployable models, lower inference costs, and more of the control loop running locally. The first product’s multiple is the entry point, not the ceiling. As model architectures converge and our expertise deepens, we specialize more aggressively: the projected gains continue through roughly 8× once the memory system is rebuilt around the model [T], and push toward a physics limit near 35–47× — the FP4 arithmetic wall. At that limit the weights themselves are etched into the silicon, the bet Taalas is making for language models [X]. Each step’s mechanism, likely range, and ceiling are set out below and on the simulation page.

Full edge inference will require model efficiency and hardware efficiency to improve together. As world models become smaller through distillation, quantization, token reduction, caching, and better architectures, we intend to remain several steps ahead of general-purpose platforms at every stage of that transition. That near-term lead wins deployments and gives us the workload knowledge, software stack, and customer relationships needed for the larger opportunity. When the model architecture finally stabilizes, we will be positioned to co-design the model and chip as a single system — creating an accelerator that NVIDIA’s general-purpose architecture cannot match.

1. The workload arrived before the chip did#

Leading humanoid robotics programs are converging on a common architecture: a video-action diffusion transformer predicts a multi-second future trajectory and replans several times per second [X: DreamZero]. The resulting trajectory is streamed to the robot and used as a reference by its lower-level control stack. At the model scales and inference rates required for capable general-purpose behavior, these systems still cannot run on the edge. As a result, teams have been forced to build extraordinary infrastructure around remote inference — including low-latency video transport, datacenter accelerators, trajectory streaming, delay compensation, and local fallback control.

The critical system-level metric is reaction latency: the elapsed time between capturing an observation and executing the corresponding action. Human visuomotor reaction time is roughly 200 milliseconds, and delays substantially beyond this range are readily perceived as sluggishness or hesitation. For a robot, this “glass-to-action” latency includes every stage of the pipeline: sensor capture and onboard IPC or bus transfers; transmission of camera frames and proprioceptive state to the datacenter; the model’s inference loop; transmission of the generated trajectory back to the robot; and the final onboard processing required before the command reaches the control loop.

Remote inference introduces more than a fixed delay. Network latency and, more importantly, latency variation can produce irregular motion unless the system employs effective buffering, local stabilization, and delay compensation. Every robot exhibits some tracking error between commanded and realized motion, but remote inference adds a second source of error: each action chunk may be generated from observations and action history that are already stale — and stale by a different amount on every cycle — by the time the command is executed.

During that delay, both the robot and its environment continue to evolve. The model’s predicted rollout can therefore diverge from the physical state in which the trajectory is ultimately applied. In action-chunked or autoregressive systems, these inconsistencies can propagate across successive predictions, creating discontinuities, drift, or even instability in ways that average latency alone does not reveal.

Techniques such as real-time chunking attempt to smooth transitions between successive trajectories through trajectory inpainting or temporal blending, while the downstream controller can damp abrupt changes before they reach the actuators [X]. These mechanisms can reduce visible transients, but they cannot reconstruct changes in the world that occurred after the original observations were captured. They also introduce a fundamental tradeoff between smoothness and reactivity: stronger blending, buffering, and damping make motion more stable, but reduce the robot’s ability to respond quickly to new information.

Wireless reliability creates an additional bottleneck. Wi-Fi can exhibit substantial jitter, intermittent packet loss, and abrupt degradation as the robot moves through a building. A system that performs well near an access point may fail when the robot enters a basement, passes through a doorway, or encounters network congestion. During evaluations of Sunday Robotics’ autonomous ACT-2 policy in real homes, unpredictable Wi-Fi latency and network jitter reportedly emerged as major bottlenecks and failure modes, motivating a shift toward local, edge-compute control [X]. The broader lesson is that a robot intended to operate autonomously in unstructured environments cannot treat continuous datacenter connectivity as a dependable part of its control loop.

2. Win the transition to edge inference#

The workload we must serve today is already extreme. Our reference point is the Wan2.1-I2V-14B / DreamZero class of video-action diffusion transformer: roughly 14 billion parameters, 40 layers, 40 attention heads, a hidden width of 5,120, and an FFN width of 13,824. Each latent frame contributes approximately 1,560 tokens, with a rolling two-second context of roughly 18,720 tokens and another 1,560–3,120 tokens introduced on each update. The system must execute one to three denoising steps, often across a guidance pair, and return a new action trajectory within a 200 ms control-cycle budget.

That workload cannot yet run inside the power, memory, and thermal envelope of a humanoid robot head. Four-bit weights alone occupy approximately 7.0 GB, while the FP8 KV window contributes another 7.7 GB. Both must be streamed from external memory on every step. The computation is similarly demanding: self-attention accounts for approximately 40.7 % of the FLOPs, the FFN 30.0 %, QKV projection 16.7 %, cross-attention 7.0 %, and output projection 5.6 % [S]. With guidance fan-out, a single step reads approximately 14.8 GB of model state before feeding both branches from the same weights.

The eventual objective is to run this entire control workload locally. Reaching that point requires two trends to converge. First, the models must become dramatically smaller and more efficient through better architectures, distillation, quantization, caching, and reduced-step generation. Second, edge accelerators must continue improving in compute density, memory bandwidth, and energy efficiency — the continuation of the hardware scaling trend often described as Jensen’s law. We believe these curves will meet. Our goal is to build the hardware platform positioned to capture that moment when they do.

But we do not need to wait for full edge autonomy to build a valuable company. Our near-term objective is more concrete: maintain a substantial performance multiple over general-purpose robotics platforms such as NVIDIA Thor on the workloads that matter for world-model inference. That advantage can take the form of higher trajectory refresh rates, larger deployable models, lower power, lower latency, or some combination of all four. Even before the entire model fits inside the robot, a purpose-built accelerator can reduce datacenter requirements, improve remote-inference economics, enable more capable onboard fallbacks, and move progressively larger portions of the control loop onto the edge. Sustaining that performance lead is the immediate engineering target and the basis on which we can win the market. The simulation page puts numbers on that lead: at the same power, our current design points put the RPU module at roughly 2.9× the Thor module’s speed with a first-generation compiler, rising past 4× as the compiler matures, measured against Thor’s dense-compute rating, the only one our no-sparsity workload can use. Our frozen success bars (2× solidly, in every mode) are unchanged and sit well below these estimates. The full published range runs from 1.9× if every uncertainty breaks against us to 15.7× with every input at its favorable end — 22× if gated low-voltage circuit techniques prove out. The top of the range is not a forecast; it is the north star the design steers toward. The full ladder, with each number’s standing:

The long-term opportunity is larger. Once the model architecture stabilizes, the distinction between model design and chip design begins to disappear. We can co-design the network and the accelerator together: fixing dataflows, specializing memory movement, fusing repeated operations, eliminating unnecessary precision and programmability, and ultimately etching the structure of the neural network into the hardware itself. The result would not merely be a faster processor running a world model. It would be an ASIC whose architecture is the world model — an accelerator that a general-purpose platform cannot economically match.

That is the destination, not the first product. The immediate task is to build a practical accelerator that stays several steps ahead of Thor while preserving enough architectural flexibility to follow a rapidly evolving model layer. By winning the intermediate stages of the transition, we earn the deployments, workload knowledge, compiler stack, and silicon experience required to build the fully co-designed system when the model architecture is ready.

3. At 40 watts, power is the wall — not memory bandwidth#

The datacenter and the edge are constrained by opposite things. In a datacenter, power is abundant and compute throughput has grown faster than memory bandwidth for a decade — large-model serving there is memory-bandwidth-bound, and the innovation race is HBM stacks, KV-cache management, and batching. A robot head inverts every term of that. This workload is intensely compute-dense — roughly 16,000 FLOPs per byte fetched, far above any edge processor’s ridge point [S] — the batch is one, and the binding limit is the ~40 W the neck can shed [S]. At the edge the scarce resource is not bytes per second; it is joules. Chunk time is whichever of four bounds is worst:

t = max( t_compute, t_memory, t_comm, E_chunk / (TDP · (1 − f_static)) )

On Thor and on our design alike, the fourth one wins by an order of magnitude [S]. So adding peak FLOPS without reducing joules per FLOP does nothing whatsoever.

When two chips are both energy-limited, throughput cancels out of the comparison entirely and you are left with:

S = η · (TDP_dut / TDP_base)

η is how much less energy our datapath spends per FLOP than the GPU we calibrated against [S]. We give our module the same 40 W as the Thor module, so the TDP ratio is one and S = η exactly: at η = 1 the two modules tie, and every point of efficiency is a point of speed. The whole product question is that one number.

We pushed the uncertainty through Monte Carlo — token count, how far the schedule compresses, the precision prior — and the number that falls out is η* ≈ 2.02 for a median 2× against Thor at the same power. The back-of-envelope agrees: 2 × 40/40 = 2 [S]. The bar itself is 2×, frozen in a machine-readable contract before design-space exploration; because a median is a coin flip we verify it at the 5th percentile in every mode, which in the current model corresponds to η ≥ 2.15, and the target of 3 survives even if we hand Thor 50 W [S]. Measured against Thor’s full 130 W bench rating the design lands at 0.66–0.92×. The RPU is a head part, and we would rather say that than quote the flattering comparison [S].

Is that reachable? TPUv4 got 1.6–3.2× performance per watt over an A100 on the same process node, on measured power [X: ISCA 2023]. Our bar sits in the middle of that range and our target at the top of it. Neither asks for anything nobody has built.

η is achievable because general-purpose processors spend surprisingly little of their power on the arithmetic itself. Hameed et al. found that only about 6 % of a general-purpose chip’s energy reaches the functional units doing useful computation; most of the rest is consumed by instruction fetch and decode, scheduling, register files, and moving data. Even aggressive specialization within a programmable architecture raised that share to only about 35 % [X]. Fabricated DNN accelerators fall between these extremes, at roughly 3–11 % [X]. The multiplier claimed in this paper comes from a simple idea: within the same 40 W power budget, our architecture directs a much larger fraction of that power to the multipliers doing useful work.

The detail that matters is where the rest goes, and what closes the gap: pipeline and clocking (22 % of their ledger), caches (19 %), register files (10 %), control (10 %) — and it is custom storage wired directly to the functional units that recovers it, not removing instructions and not wider SIMD [X: ISCA 2010]. Tensor cores already did the fused-matmul part, so our headroom is everything around the multiplier.

Attention is where this gets awkward. Because attention runs at FP8 while the linear layers run at FP4, roughly 62 % of the arithmetic energy is attention and 37 % is matmul and projections [S]. (Byte movement is a separate, system-level category, not part of this arithmetic split: roughly 4–5 % of chunk energy under the launch accounting, rising toward a third of chunk energy as the arithmetic improves — which is exactly why the 3D-memory lever is priced under “Beyond this design” in Section 5, not here.) The cause of the split is precision, not data movement: self-attention is 40.7 % of the arithmetic but 73.3 % of the arithmetic energy, because cost scales with the square of operand width. Set matmul energy to exactly zero and the speedup still caps at 1.59×, so there is no version of this chip that wins on matrix multiply alone.

Fused streaming attention is not a separate advantage stacked on top of a TPU-style control path. FlashAttention has been the PyTorch default since 2.0 and our measured baseline uses it; Blackwell has native FP8 and native FP4. A GPU running this workload already fuses its attention and already has both formats. What is left is implementation efficiency rather than a new algorithm: a GPU still alternates between tensor cores for the two matrix multiplies and vector units for the exponential and the softmax, losing utilization at every handoff, and keys and values still travel through shared memory and the register file. Those gains are real, but modest.

The result is a simpler and more testable thesis. There is no separate attention multiplier to add to a control-path advantage. Everything reduces to one ratio: the share of energy each design delivers to its multipliers. The attention datapath is where that share has to be earned, competing against every other decision for the same budget, rather than a bonus on top.

The remaining memory-bandwidth risk

There is one important inconsistency in our current analysis. Profiling shows that WAN-class video models are limited by memory bandwidth on a B200 [F]. Our simulator, however, predicts that the same workload would be limited primarily by compute on the proposed accelerator. We think there are three reasons for the difference.

First, GPUs move much more intermediate data than a fully fused accelerator would. A conventional, unfused attention implementation can write roughly 2.3 GB of attention scores per layer — about 25 times the total data movement assumed by our fused pipeline. On a GPU, whether those intermediate values can be eliminated depends on kernel fusion, compiler behavior, and hardware utilization. On a purpose-built chip, the fused dataflow would be part of the architecture itself [S].

Second, batch-one inference is particularly inefficient for GPUs. Because the workload processes one control request at a time, there is little opportunity to reuse weights across a batch. The GPU must repeatedly fetch a large model for relatively little computation, which can move the workload from the compute-bound side of the roofline to the bandwidth-bound side.

Third, our simulator has not yet been calibrated against a workload that is known to be memory-bound. This is the least validated part of the model, and therefore the part we trust least. Adding detailed B200 profiling is a gate 1 requirement, not an optional refinement [F]. (The gates — what each one settles, costs, and what kills it — are laid out on the business page.)

This matters because the current design does not have a large margin. It sits only about 1.5× on the compute-bound side of the roofline crossover, and more frequent refreshes of the most recent tokens consume much of that margin [S]. A moderate error in our bandwidth assumptions could therefore change the system from compute-bound to memory-bound.

If that happens, the available fixes are well understood. Lossless compression of the weight stream could reduce bandwidth demand to roughly 62–83 % of its current level, and hardware capable of decoding one weight per clock has already been demonstrated [X]. A 512-bit LPDDR5X interface can provide roughly 614 GB/s, while current Apple systems already deliver about 546 GB/s of on-package bandwidth. LPDDR6 should provide substantially more bandwidth per package. Longer term, processing-in-memory could perform some KV-cache operations inside the memory banks, while hybrid-bonded 3D DRAM could reduce the energy cost of moving each bit [X].

Even under a conservative configuration — FP8 weights and no aggressive quantization — the model would stream approximately 15.5 GB per denoising step. Our current estimate is that this still fits beneath the compute time when paired with a 512-bit LPDDR5X interface [S]. However, compute area and memory-interface area trade directly against one another. Adding more MAC units is not useful if the memory system cannot feed them, while adding unnecessary memory channels increases area, power, and packaging complexity.

For that reason, the final balance between compute and bandwidth will be set at gate 1, after we have calibrated the simulator against real B200 profiling. The worst case appears manageable: it would require a 512-bit memory interface and lossless weight compression, but not an unproven memory technology or aggressive quantization [F].

So the unresolved question is not whether a solution exists. We have practical options across the range of bandwidth requirements we currently consider plausible. The remaining uncertainty is which option the real workload actually needs. That is a measurement problem, and obtaining that measurement is one of the main objectives of gate 1.

4. Build the simulator before the silicon#

We cannot fabricate the target hardware yet, so our first job is to build the most credible simulation environment possible. The simulator gives us a way to explore architectures, reject weak ideas, and quantify tradeoffs before committing millions of dollars to silicon. But a simulator is only useful if it can reproduce hardware that already exists.

We began by calibrating it against an RTX PRO 6000 Blackwell workstation. We ran the actual target workload using the same tensor geometries and one-, two-, and three-step denoising configurations assumed elsewhere in this design. For each trajectory chunk, we measured execution time directly and sampled board power through nvidia-smi [M]. We then asked the simulator to predict those same runs without access to the measured results.

Across the tested configurations, the simulator came within 0.5–3.9 % on execution time and 0.8–2.5 % on energy [M]. We are also working with experts in accelerator architecture, physical design, memory systems, compilers, and robotics inference to challenge the simulator’s assumptions and validate the ideas derived from it. Their role is not merely to confirm the design, but to identify where simplified models omit real implementation constraints: routing congestion, utilization losses, synchronization overhead, power delivery, thermal limits, memory-controller behavior, and the cost of preserving programmability. The simulator is therefore not a substitute for domain expertise; it is a shared quantitative framework through which those experts can test and refine the architecture.

Direct measurement rules out two approaches that look attractive on paper [M]:

  1. Running wider at a lower clock does not automatically save energy. On the measured GPU, energy per FLOP remains approximately flat from 1.0 to 3.1 GHz after subtracting idle power. A meaningful gain would require custom low-voltage or sub-Vmin circuit design; it cannot be obtained simply by selecting a lower operating point on existing silicon.
  2. Precision scaling delivers less than ideal arithmetic ratios suggest. Moving from FP16 to FP8 produced a measured speedup of 1.77–1.82×, rather than the theoretical 2×. We therefore model FP16-to-FP4 improvement using a measured prior of 2.6–3.4×, well below the roughly 10× that ideal arithmetic ratios would suggest.

5. What we would build#

The RPU: a head-resident inference engine, sized to the shapes above. This is the design point our instrument currently scores — the existence proof behind the first-silicon numbers, chosen by the co-design machinery and revised by it as the model landscape moves, not a frozen commitment. Targets unless tagged otherwise [T]:

  • About 1.9 M FP4 multiply-accumulate units at ~1.05 GHz (4 PF dense) in 128×128 weight-streaming systolic tiles. The model's widths divide the array exactly: 5120 = 8 × 640, and 13 824 = 8 × 1728.
  • FP8 multiply into FP32 accumulators held in dedicated SRAM beneath the array — the narrow-multiply, wide-accumulate pattern from TPUv1 [X].
  • A 256-bit LPDDR5X interface at 307 GB/s and at least 16 GB. The prefetcher is a counter, because the chip reads the identical ~44 GB address sequence every single chunk. About 90 MB of on-die SRAM holds stream buffers and the activation spine, and never a resident set of weights.
  • A KV ring that lives in DRAM and advances by pointer at chunk end. Weights and context are fetched once per step and fanned out to both guidance branches in silicon.
  • Refresh gets its own slot. The sequencer issues per-bank DRAM refresh in idle windows it already knows about. Left alone, hot-head refresh derating costs 14–28 % of effective bandwidth and adds tail latency; scheduling it removes the interference by construction [S].
  • No operating system, and no instructions. The same chunk costs about 1 200 kernel launches on a GPU and zero in-band instructions here. Being predictable is what makes it fast, not a tax on speed: under a 7 ms p99 bound the original TPU held 80 % of peak while a same-generation K80 fell to 37 % [X: ISCA 2017]. Our 200 ms loop is tighter than that benchmark, not looser.
  • A schedule ROM with 1-, 2- and 3-step modes, produced offline by a tool that reuses the calibrated simulator as its cost model and emits a worst-case deadline certificate per mode. Weights, scales, token counts and schedules load from an image; tile geometry and number formats are fixed in the mask.
  • A small programmable block, about 2 % of the datapath, for flow-ODE or CEM-style planning. Supporting a JEPA-family workload costs 1.9 % of DreamZero-path performance per watt — both families stream 7 GB of weights, so no memory tier separates them [S].

The requirement is three denoising steps with guidance. At η = 2.15 — the 5th-percentile-every-mode criterion for the 2× bar — against Thor at the same 40 W [S], the RPU lands a 2.13× win. At the design target of 3 the median reaches 2.95×, and in its highest-quality planning mode the chip finishes a two-second imagined trajectory in 4.5 s where Thor needs 13 [S]. The relative bar clears.

We do not clear an absolute 200 ms — the model puts that at η ≈ 12, which is outside every evidence band we have. Closing that gap is not a silicon problem alone; the model-side half of the answer is a companion note, The model has to meet us halfway. How far the denoising schedule distils — one step at reduced token count with task success intact — is the lever that moves absolute latency rather than the ratio, and it decides whether 5 Hz is ever reachable.

Heat removal is part of the design

Every watt the neck can move multiplies speed directly, so we treat the thermal path as a component of the chip rather than packaging. The etched schedule gives the package a power trace known at compile time: the guardband shrinks to manufacturing variation, and no governor throttles mid-chunk. The die spreads its 40 watts across roughly 450 mm², under 9 W/cm² with no hotspots, which is what keeps the head fanless [T].

Cooling the robot itself provides — cold plates under 3 mm are already mainstream in humanoids, and heat pipes can move rejection into a torso radiator — raises the ceiling for any chip equally, so we score it symmetrically and claim none of it as η [S]. What survives asymmetrically: the guardband, the flat power density, and shipping the reference thermal design with the part. Die-embedded microfluidic cooling, which only a chip designer can build in, is the gated option that would make 3D-stacked DRAM thermally survivable [X].

Still open

Each of these is open, each one matters, and each comes with a way to tell whether you are making progress.

  1. The attention energy path. Design the streamer that makes 62 % of dynamic energy cheap: division-free online softmax, fused exponential-multiply, static maximum bounds, and the right split of dataflow across fabric passes. Section 3 is the argument that the thesis fails without this.
  2. The dataflow question. Weight-stationary against output-stationary against broadcast-tree, on our exact shapes, pass by pass. A stall-free conveyor removes output-stationary's usual advantage, and nobody has published this comparison for chunk diffusion.
  3. The energy ledger. Build the tile-level model and produce our own version of Hameed's Table 3: millijoules per chunk, per component. This one can kill the project cheaply — mapped η below 2.2, or functional units below 35 % of energy, and we stop before anyone spends tapeout money.
  4. Sub-Vmin operation. Etched claims half-voltage math blocks in A0 silicon, and the near-threshold literature offers split voltage domains and 8T SRAM. Razor-style margin recovery, quoted around 47 %, does not hold up: it measures recovery against signoff margins rather than energy per inference, published silicon shows efficiency actually falling once you push below the voltage floor, and it recovers errors by re-running work, which makes latency variable — unacceptable when the whole point is a provable deadline. The technique that has genuinely shipped in production processors is a monitor circuit driving per-tile voltage adjustment. On sizing, measured silicon for a real accelerator with memory and interconnect gives about 3.3×, against roughly 9× for the idealised simulations usually cited; once the memory interface is included, which runs at a fixed voltage and cannot be scaled at all, this is worth perhaps 20 % at the system level. Prove it on a test tile or leave it out of η permanently.
  5. The robot memory engine. Persistent latent state, episodic retrieval, fast-weight layers, bounded context — in hardware. A write-capable weight path breaks the read-only conveyor assumption, so this has to be scoped before RTL freezes that assumption in.
  6. The compiler. A scheduling tool today; a PyTorch→StableHLO→MLIR backend as the platform grows. The moat is compiler plus scheduling plus memory hierarchy, and the cost model for it already exists as the calibrated simulator.
  7. Whether HBM comes back. We ruled it out early on power grounds and built the entire memory argument around LPDDR. If the etched datapath frees enough of the budget, HBM may be affordable after all — and it would retire the bandwidth question outright. That assumption predates the calibrated model and has never been re-tested against it.

Beyond this design

These sit beyond the current design and count toward no success bar until demonstrated, in rough order of distance; the 3D-DRAM and co-design steps are priced in the ladder in Section 2.

  • Conventional architecture gains — none left on the table. Every commercial design that deviates from our design point (grids of small cores, reconfigurable fabrics, exposed datapaths) pays for its flexibility in measured efficiency [S].
  • On-package memory. Our perfectly sequential traffic permits many slow, short-reach channels — the physics that makes on-package memory several times cheaper per bit. Worth perhaps 10–40 % at system level; the interconnect between arrays is the one term that could subtract rather than add, so we price it before any array-size decision [S].
  • 3D DRAM — the optimized level. Hybrid-bonded memory stacked on the compute die drops each weight read from roughly 40 to 8 pJ/byte and moves the memory roofline that otherwise caps compute gains near 2×. Rigorous upper bound of about 20–31×, or 29–44× compounded with gated circuit techniques [T]. Three desk questions gate it: thermal survival in a fanless head, capacity outside custom supply agreements, and hybrid-bond reliability under vibration.

What these lock in: the near extensions commit us to nothing about the model. Three-dimensional stacking deepens a bet we have already made on the transformer attention chain, and would not serve a post-transformer architecture as gracefully — a deeper version of an existing bet, not a new one, and we state it rather than discover it later [S].
