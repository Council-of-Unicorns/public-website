A measured case for etching world-action-model inference into a robot’s head

The ChatGPT moment for robotics requires running robot policies on the edge.

Source tags: [M] measured ourselves · [S] computed by our simulator · [F] measured in production at a robotics company · [X] published elsewhere · [T] a target, not yet reached.

Executive summary#

Humanoid robotics is converging on video-action diffusion transformers that predict several seconds of motion and must replan fast enough to achieve human-like reaction latency. Today’s leading models are still too large to run inside a robot head, forcing teams to rely on kilowatt-class datacenter GPUs and continuous network connections. That puts latency, Wi-Fi jitter, and connectivity failures directly inside the robot’s control loop [F].

Our first product targets 5× Thor’s performance at the same 40 W [T]. Our simulation puts first silicon at 2.7–6.7×, with a 2.9× central estimate [S], so 5× is aggressive but still within the modeled range. That gain would immediately support stronger robot demos, larger on-device models, lower inference cost, and more local control.

The upside grows as the model stabilizes. Rebuilding the memory system around the workload could push gains toward ~8× [T], while aggressive codesign approaches a 35–47× FP4 physics limit by hardening stable weights and computation into silicon, similar to Taalas’s approach for language models [X].

The bottleneck is energy efficiency, not memory bandwidth#

The datacenter and the edge are solving almost opposite engineering problems. Much of modern datacenter accelerator design is organized around the memory-bandwidth wall. On datacenter racks, compute has grown faster than memory bandwidth, so AI workloads are typically memory-bound, which motivates optimizations around HBM, caching, batching, operator fusion, and packaging. However, on edge chips, the same workload becomes compute-bound due to power constraints. To address this bottleneck, we must focus on energy efficiency.

A DreamZero-class model makes the ratio concrete. Its 14 billion parameters across 40 layers require about 7×10¹⁴ operations per control chunk. Each step must also stream the model state that cannot fit on-die: 7.0 GB of 4-bit weights plus a 7.7 GB FP8 context window, or about 44 GB per chunk. That gives an arithmetic intensity of roughly 16,000 FLOPs per byte fetched [S].

Jetson Thor provides 1,035 dense FP4 TFLOP/s and 273 GB/s of memory bandwidth, putting its nominal roofline near 3,800 FLOPs/byte [X: NVIDIA Jetson Thor Series Modules datasheet]. The workload therefore sits about 4× inside the compute-bound regime on paper. Real GPU traffic can narrow that margin, but simply adding bandwidth does not address the dominant constraint in our current design.

Why a purpose-built chip can beat a GPU at 40 W

Specialization can deliver large efficiency gains: TPUv4 achieved 1.6–3.2× higher performance per watt than A100 on measured workloads at the same process node [X: ISCA 2023]. The opportunity is reducing the overhead of programmability.

Hameed et al. found that only about 6 % of a general-purpose processor’s energy reached its functional units, with much of the rest spent on clocking, caches, register files, control, and data movement [X: ISCA 2010]. Batch-one robot-policy inference is unusually easy to specialize: the graph, tensor shapes, operator order, and memory-access pattern are largely fixed, yet our workload launches roughly 1,200 GPU kernels per trajectory chunk [S]. An RPU can hard-code that schedule, stream weights predictably, keep intermediates local, and tailor the memory hierarchy to the workload.

The same overhead appears in memory traffic. WAN-class video models can become memory-bandwidth-bound even on B200 [F] because real execution moves more data than the fused minimum. An unfused attention path moves roughly 25× more data than our fused model [S], while batch-one inference must refetch weights for every request.
