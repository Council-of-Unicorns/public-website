Whitepaper — July 2026

A measured case for etching world-model inference into a robot’s head

A humanoid's world model has to produce an action every 200 milliseconds, and it has to do it from inside the robot's head. Nothing can do that today. A datacenter GPU can hit the deadline at a kilowatt and a thousand miles away; a radio link can carry the answer back until the moment the channel gets busy. This is our case that a chip can do it in the head instead — and, just as importantly, the places where that case is still thin.

Every number below says where it came from. [M] we measured it ourselves. [S] our simulator computed it. [F] we measured it in production at a robotics company. [X] someone else published it. [T] it is a target we have not hit yet. An untagged number is a bug.

The short version

A humanoid's control loop now runs on a 14-billion-parameter diffusion transformer that has to answer every 200 milliseconds. Today that means a kilowatt datacenter GPU, or a radio link — so either facility power or Wi-Fi jitter ends up inside the loop that keeps the robot upright [F].

We wanted to know whether a chip in the head could do it instead. So we measured where the time and energy actually go, operator by operator, and built a model that has to reproduce real Blackwell silicon to within 0.7–4.6 % before we let it predict anything [M].

The answer surprised us. At head power, peak FLOPS stops mattering. The chip is limited by how fast it can turn joules into work, so the entire comparison collapses to a single number: how much less energy our datapath spends per FLOP than a GPU does. Call it η. We score both chips at the same 40 watts, so the comparison is exact: the speed ratio is the efficiency ratio. Beating Thor by 2× on the median needs η ≈ 2.05; holding that 2× at the pessimistic end of the uncertainty needs 2.15; and our design target of 3 keeps the win even if Thor's real head budget turns out 25 % better than we assume [S]. The whole range sits inside the 1.6–3.2× that TPUv4 actually demonstrated against an A100 [X]. So it is not a fantasy.

The uncomfortable part: about 62 % of that energy goes to attention, not matrix multiply [S]. Build the world's best MAC array, leave attention alone, and you still cap out at 1.17×. Attention is where this chip is won or lost.

1. The workload arrived before the chip did

The leading humanoid programs have converged on the same shape. A video-action diffusion transformer imagines the next two seconds, emits a chunk of actions, and does it again five times a second [X: DreamZero]. Miss the 200 ms window and the answer arrives after the robot has already moved.

We have built both of the answers that exist today, and neither one is a product.

Distilling the diffusion schedule from 16 steps to 3 got us to control rate — on two B200-class GPUs per robot, a kilowatt of datacenter silicon each. So we built the other answer too: move the compute off the robot, with a custom media-over-QUIC transport, guidance split across GPUs, velocity caching, graph capture, and train-time chunking [F]. It worked. But simulate the same compute over a busy Wi-Fi link and the deadline miss rate is 7.8 %, against a target of 10−4. On-head it is near zero [S]. No amount of transport engineering closes that gap, because the gap is the radio.

So the inference has to live in the head. The only silicon you can currently put there is a general-purpose SoC, and held to head power it runs this workload about 20× too slow [S].

2. The workload never changes shape

We size the chip against Wan2.1-I2V-14B / DreamZero geometry. Fourteen billion parameters, forty layers, forty heads, a hidden width of 5120 and an FFN of 13 824. Each latent frame is 1 560 tokens, and the model keeps a rolling two seconds of them, about 18 720, while adding 1 560 to 3 120 new ones per step. One to three denoising steps, a guidance pair, and an answer due every 200 ms.

No single number there matters much on its own. What matters is that none of them change at runtime. Same shapes, same layer count, same token counts, every chunk, forever. That is what makes etching the thing possible at all.

Four-bit weights come to 7.0 GB. The FP8 KV window is another 7.7 GB. Neither fits in any SRAM you can afford at head power, so both stream from DRAM every step [S].

Where does a step go? Self-attention takes 38.7 % of the FLOPs, the FFN 28.6 %, QKV projection 15.9 %, cross-attention 10.9 %, output projection 5.3 % [S]. With guidance fan-out the step reads 14.8 GB once and feeds both branches from it; fetching twice would double that for nothing.

Arithmetic intensity lands around 17 000 FLOP per byte, far to the right of any edge device's ridge point. By the textbook definition this loop is compute-bound [S]. Section 8 is about why that comfortable margin is less comfortable than it looks.

3. At 40 watts, energy sets the clock

A robot head can push roughly 40 W out through the neck [S]. Chunk time is whichever of four bounds is worst:

t = max( t_compute, t_memory, t_comm, E_chunk / (TDP · (1 − f_static)) )

On Thor and on our design alike, the fourth one wins by an order of magnitude [S]. So adding peak FLOPS without reducing joules per FLOP does nothing whatsoever.

When two chips are both energy-limited, throughput cancels out of the comparison entirely and you are left with:

S = η · (TDP_dut / TDP_base)

η is how much less energy our datapath spends per FLOP than the GPU we calibrated against [S]. We give our chip the same 40 W as Thor, so the TDP ratio is one and S = η exactly: at η = 1 the two chips tie, and every point of efficiency is a point of speed. The whole product question is that one number.

4. We made the model earn the right to predict

Before we let the simulator say anything about a chip nobody has built, we made it predict hardware we could actually measure.

We had an RTX PRO 6000 Blackwell workstation, so we ran the real thing on it — the same tensor shapes the model uses, at one, two and three denoising steps — timing each chunk and reading board power off nvidia-smi as it went [M]. Then we asked the simulator to predict those same runs. It came within 0.7–4.6 % on time and 0.2–2.4 % on energy [M]. The fit also puts the GPU at 84 % of its theoretical compute, which is a generous figure: if it is wrong, it is wrong in the incumbent’s favour.

Point the same model at Thor or a B200 and it misses by 40–65 %. We have never had either on a bench, so those two rows are estimates, and we would rather say that than quote them [S].

Two measurements killed stories we were fond of [M]:

  1. Running wide and slow buys nothing. We expected a voltage win from dropping the clock. Energy per FLOP turns out to be flat from 1.0 to 3.1 GHz once idle power is subtracted. That win needs custom sub-Vmin design; you cannot get it by choosing an operating point on existing silicon.
  2. Precision scaling underdelivers. FP16→FP8 measures 1.77–1.82×, not the 2× the arithmetic promises. We now carry FP16→FP4 as a measured prior of 2.6–3.4× instead of the roughly 10× we had assumed. That single correction tripled our absolute energy estimates.

Better we find these than someone finds them for us in a diligence meeting.

5. Where 2–3× can actually come from

We pushed the uncertainty through Monte Carlo — token count, how far the schedule compresses, the precision prior — and the number that falls out is η* ≈ 2.05 for a median 2× against Thor at the same power. The back-of-envelope agrees: 2 × 40/40 = 2 [S]. The bar we actually hold is stricter, because a median is a coin flip: 2× at the 5th percentile needs η ≥ 2.15, and the target of 3 survives even if we hand Thor 50 W [S]. Measured against Thor’s full 130 W bench rating the design lands at 0.66–0.92×. The RPU is a head part, and we would rather say that than quote the flattering comparison [S].

Is that reachable? TPUv4 got 1.6–3.2× performance per watt over an A100 on the same process node, on measured power [X: ISCA 2023]. Our bar sits in the middle of that range and our target at the top of it. Neither asks for anything nobody has built.

The more useful guidance is older. Hameed and colleagues took a general-purpose chip apart and found only 5.8 % of its energy reaching the functional units — the transistors that actually do arithmetic. Fusing custom datapaths pushes that toward 35 %, and what closes the remaining processor-to-ASIC gap is custom storage wired directly to those units. Not removing instructions, and not wider SIMD [X: ISCA 2010]. Tensor cores already did the fused-matmul part, so our headroom is everything around the multiplier: pipeline and clocking (22 % of their ledger), caches (19 %), register files (10 %), control (10 %).

Attention is where this gets awkward. Because attention runs at FP8 while the linear layers run at FP4, roughly 62 % of dynamic energy is attention, 36 % is matmul and projections, and 2 % is moving bytes [S]. Set matmul energy to exactly zero and the speedup still caps at 1.17×. There is no version of this chip that wins on matrix multiply. The work is in the attention path: streaming the softmax rather than materializing it, hiding the division, fusing the exponential and the multiply, and exploiting the fact that a fixed geometry lets us precompute a per-layer maximum [X: arXiv 2505.14201, 2505.14314].

6. What we would build

The RPU: a head-resident inference engine, sized to the shapes above. Targets unless tagged otherwise [T]:

  • About 1.9 M FP4 multiply-accumulate units at ~1.05 GHz (4 PF dense) in 128×128 weight-streaming systolic tiles. The model's widths divide the array exactly: 5120 = 8 × 640, and 13 824 = 8 × 1728.
  • FP8 multiply into FP32 accumulators held in dedicated SRAM beneath the array — the narrow-multiply, wide-accumulate pattern from TPUv1 [X].
  • A 256-bit LPDDR5X interface at 307 GB/s and at least 16 GB. The prefetcher is a counter, because the chip reads the identical ~44 GB address sequence every single chunk. About 90 MB of on-die SRAM holds stream buffers and the activation spine, and never a resident set of weights.
  • A KV ring that lives in DRAM and advances by pointer at chunk end. Weights and context are fetched once per step and fanned out to both guidance branches in silicon.
  • Refresh gets its own slot. The sequencer issues per-bank DRAM refresh in idle windows it already knows about. Left alone, hot-head refresh derating costs 14–28 % of effective bandwidth and adds tail latency; scheduling it removes the interference by construction [S].
  • No operating system, and no instructions. The same chunk costs about 1 200 kernel launches on a GPU and zero in-band instructions here. Being predictable is what makes it fast, not a tax on speed: under a 7 ms p99 bound the original TPU held 80 % of peak while a same-generation K80 fell to 37 % [X: ISCA 2017]. Our 200 ms loop is tighter than that benchmark, not looser.
  • A schedule ROM with 1-, 2- and 3-step modes, produced offline by a tool that reuses the calibrated simulator as its cost model and emits a worst-case deadline certificate per mode. Weights, scales, token counts and schedules load from an image; tile geometry and number formats are fixed in the mask.
  • A small programmable block, about 2 % of the datapath, for flow-ODE or CEM-style planning. Supporting a JEPA-family workload costs 1.9 % of DreamZero-path performance per watt — both families stream 7 GB of weights, so no memory tier separates them [S].

The requirement is three denoising steps with guidance. At the η = 2.15 bar, against Thor at the same 40 W [S], a chunk takes 6.2 s where Thor takes 13.0 s, a 2.10× win. At the design target of 3 it reaches 2.86×. The relative bar clears.

We do not clear an absolute 200 ms — the model puts that at η ≈ 12, which is outside every evidence band we have. Closing that gap is not a silicon problem alone, and the next section is what we would do about it.

Heat removal is part of the design

Every watt the neck can move multiplies speed directly, so we treat the thermal path as a component of the chip rather than packaging. The etched schedule gives the package a power trace known at compile time: the guardband shrinks to manufacturing variation, and no governor throttles mid-chunk. The die spreads its 40 watts across roughly 450 mm², under 9 W/cm² with no hotspots, which is what keeps the head fanless [T].

Cooling the robot itself provides — cold plates under 3 mm are already mainstream in humanoids, and heat pipes can move rejection into a torso radiator — raises the ceiling for any chip equally, so we score it symmetrically and claim none of it as η [S]. What survives asymmetrically: the guardband, the flat power density, and shipping the reference thermal design with the part. Die-embedded microfluidic cooling, which only a chip designer can build in, is the gated option that would make 3D-stacked DRAM thermally survivable [X].

7. The model has to meet us halfway

Silicon alone does not close the gap to 200 ms, so we went through the workload optimizations one at a time and checked each against its own literature rather than its abstract. Below is what each one actually buys, as a multiplier on chunk energy. These are the multipliers on the three-step schedule the requirement assumes [T].

Compose those, discount for the work they duplicate, and the range is roughly 1.7–3.8× [T]. Two caveats belong with that number rather than after it. Every lever is gated on task success: one that saves energy and drops the robot is not a lever. And the range is composed with 2:4 sparsity and FP4 KV switched on, which the constraint below holds in reserve. Read it as the ceiling with everything enabled, not the figure to plan against.

Cross-chunk reuse is the one we would build first. It is the largest single multiplier, and it survives few-step schedules where per-step caching does not. No language-model accelerator has any reason to build it, which leaves it to us. The published numbers are encouraging: WorldCache reports 2.3× at 99.4 % quality on a video world model, and Chorus reports 1.45× on 4-step distilled models [X].

There is a catch worth stating plainly. The schedule still has to reserve the worst-case slot, so reuse saves energy without making the deadline proof any stronger. It only counts toward the deadline if training can guarantee a floor on how much overlap there will be — and train-time chunking is the tool that could train that floor in [F].

One constraint bounds the whole section: the control policy must not depend on aggressive quantization — no INT2 weights, no FP4 KV, no 2:4 pruning [F]. Those stay last resorts, behind everything bit-exact and structural.

8. The memory wall, honestly

Here is a contradiction we have not fully resolved. WAN-class video models measure bandwidth-bound on a B200 [F], while our fused model calls the same loop compute-bound. Three things reconcile it.

First, unfused attention writes out 2.3 GB of scores per layer — about 25× our entire stream. A GPU's memory-boundedness here is partly manufactured by imperfect fusion, and an etched pipeline makes the fused byte count a guarantee rather than a hope [S]. Second, batch-1 execution destroys weight reuse on a GPU, pushing the operating point across the roofline crossover. Third, and least comfortably: our instrument has no memory-bound anchor yet, which makes its memory-regime numbers the least trustworthy thing in it. Ingesting real B200 profiling is a gate-1 requirement, not a nice-to-have [F].

The candidate configuration sits only 1.5× on the compute side of its ridge, and hot-head refresh nearly eats that margin [S]. If it closes, the ladder out is ordinary engineering rather than invention: lossless entropy coding of the weight stream cuts demand to 0.62–0.83× with one-weight-per-clock decoders already demonstrated [X]; 512-bit LPDDR5X reaches 614 GB/s, and Apple ships 546 GB/s on-package today; LPDDR6 is about 2.25× per package; LPDDR6-PIM can run KV passes inside the bank, and both Samsung and SK hynix are standardizing it; hybrid-bonded 3D DRAM reaches 0.66–0.88 pJ per bit for a second generation [X]. A conservative profile with FP8 weights and no aggressive quantization streams about 15.5 GB per step and still fits inside the compute shadow at 512-bit LPDDR5X [S]. MAC area trades roughly linearly against PHY channels, so we settle the compute-to-bandwidth ratio at gate 1, against a real anchor, before it is expensive to change.

9. What is still unsolved

Each of these is open, each one matters, and each comes with a way to tell whether you are making progress.

  1. The attention energy path. Design the streamer that makes 62 % of dynamic energy cheap: division-free online softmax, fused exponential-multiply, static maximum bounds, and the right split of dataflow across fabric passes. Section 5 is the argument that the thesis fails without this.
  2. The dataflow question. Weight-stationary against output-stationary against broadcast-tree, on our exact shapes, pass by pass. A stall-free conveyor removes output-stationary's usual advantage, and nobody has published this comparison for chunk diffusion.
  3. The energy ledger. Build the tile-level model and produce our own version of Hameed's Table 3: millijoules per chunk, per component. This one can kill the project cheaply — mapped η below 2.2, or functional units below 35 % of energy, and we stop before anyone spends tapeout money.
  4. The memory-bound anchor. Ingest real B200 profiling, identify the realized bandwidth-utilization coefficient, locate the crossover, and set the ratio of MACs to memory channels.
  5. Sub-Vmin operation. Etched claims half-voltage math blocks in A0 silicon, and the near-threshold literature offers split domains, 8T SRAM, and Razor-style margin recovery measured around 47 %. Prove it on a test tile or leave it out of η permanently.
  6. A certifiable reuse floor. Co-design train-time chunking with the scheduler so cross-chunk reuse carries a trained, provable minimum. That converts a heuristic into deadline credit.
  7. The robot memory engine. Persistent latent state, episodic retrieval, fast-weight layers, bounded context — in hardware. A write-capable weight path breaks the read-only conveyor assumption, so this has to be scoped before RTL freezes that assumption in.
  8. The compiler. A scheduling tool today; a PyTorch→StableHLO→MLIR backend as the platform grows. The moat is compiler plus scheduling plus memory hierarchy, and the cost model for it already exists as the calibrated simulator.
  9. Whether HBM comes back. We ruled it out early on power grounds and built the entire memory argument around LPDDR. If the etched datapath frees enough of the budget, HBM may be affordable after all — and it would retire the bandwidth question outright. That assumption predates the calibrated model and has never been re-tested against it.
  10. How far the schedule distils. Getting to one step at reduced token count with task success intact is unproven. It is the lever that moves absolute latency rather than the ratio, so it decides whether 5 Hz is ever reachable.

11. Why this stays defensible

The socket is the humanoid head. You win it per robot generation, and you defend it with certification — deterministic timing, once it is inside somebody's safety case, is a moat measured in years rather than months.

It is worth being explicit about why we price the bet on the ratio rather than on absolute real-time. World models are getting smaller quickly — 2.3 B in LaWAM, 1 B in Efficient-WAM, 500 M in SmolVLA [X]. Absolute feasibility arrives eventually on its own, for us and for NVIDIA alike. What does not arrive on its own is energy per FLOP. Measured GPU performance per watt doubles roughly every three to four years, far slower than the headline throughput figure, and inside a fixed 40 W head you cannot spend your way past it [X]. η comes from deleting generality, and a process node is available to both parties, so the ratio survives what the absolute number does not.

Each competitor misses one constraint, and it is a different one each time. Thor stays general because CUDA is the product. Datacenter transformer ASICs are 20× off in power regime. Edge NPUs are roughly 100× too small for a 14 B streaming model. The relative bar, 2× Thor in-head, rides entirely on η. The absolute 5 Hz loop is a joint model-and-silicon program, and we say so in public rather than only in private.

12. How we work

  1. Measure before extrapolating. No prediction about silicon that does not exist until the model reproduces silicon that does. That gate blocked every feasibility claim we wanted to make until real anchors landed, and it still governs.
  2. Adverse results ship. The flat-DVFS result, the 1.77× precision number, and the corrected 5 Hz claim are all in the record with the same prominence as the numbers we like.
  3. Say where every number came from. Untagged numbers are bugs.
  4. A gate that cannot fail is theatre. Ours name the number, the threshold, and what dies when it misses.
  5. One instrument, one set of rules. The same utilization model scores every hardware row. Our design never gets a friendlier assumption than the incumbent.

If that method and the problems in Section 9 read like the job you want, the instrument, the data and the open questions are here. The silicon is waiting on you.
