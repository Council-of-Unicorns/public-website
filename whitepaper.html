Whitepaper — August 2026

A measured case for etching world-model inference into a robot’s head

The ChatGPT moment for robotics requires world models to run on the robot.

Source tags: [M] measured ourselves · [S] computed by our simulator · [F] measured in production at a robotics company · [X] published elsewhere · [T] a target, not yet reached.

Executive summary#

Humanoid robotics is converging on video-action diffusion transformers that predict several seconds of motion and must replan fast enough to achieve human-like reaction latency. Today’s leading models are still too large to run inside a robot head, forcing teams to rely on kilowatt-class datacenter GPUs and continuous network connections. That puts latency, Wi-Fi jitter, and connectivity failures directly inside the robot’s control loop [F].

Our first-product target is aggressive: 5× faster than NVIDIA Thor at the same 40-watt power budget [T]. The calibrated instrument brackets first silicon at 2.7–6.7× with a 2.9× central design point [S] — the target sits deliberately in the favorable half of that range. That advantage creates an immediate competitive edge: more compelling robot demos, larger deployable models, lower inference costs, and more of the control loop running locally. The first product’s multiple is the entry point, not the ceiling. As model architectures converge and our expertise deepens, we specialize more aggressively: the projected gains continue through roughly 8× once the memory system is rebuilt around the model [T], and push toward a physics limit near 35–47× — the FP4 arithmetic wall. At that limit the weights themselves are etched into the silicon, the bet Taalas is making for language models [X]. Each step’s mechanism, likely range, and ceiling are set out below and on the simulation page.

Full edge inference will require model efficiency and hardware efficiency to improve together. As world models become smaller through distillation, quantization, token reduction, caching, and better architectures, we intend to remain several steps ahead of general-purpose platforms at every stage of that transition. That near-term lead wins deployments and gives us the workload knowledge, software stack, and customer relationships needed for the larger opportunity. When the model architecture finally stabilizes, we will be positioned to co-design the model and chip as a single system — creating an accelerator that NVIDIA’s general-purpose architecture cannot match.

1. The workload arrived before the chip did#

State-of-the-art world-action models cannot run in real time on edge GPUs. Therefore, teams have been forced to build extraordinary infrastructure around remote inference — including low-latency video transport, datacenter accelerators, trajectory streaming, delay compensation, and local fallback control. But remote inference can never scale beyond the demo.

The critical system-level metric is reaction latency: the elapsed time between capturing an observation and executing the corresponding action. Human visuomotor reaction time is roughly 200 milliseconds, and delays substantially beyond this range are readily perceived as sluggishness or hesitation. For a robot, this “glass-to-action” latency includes every stage of the pipeline: sensor capture and onboard IPC or bus transfers; transmission of camera frames and proprioceptive state to the datacenter; the model’s inference loop; transmission of the generated trajectory back to the robot; and the final onboard processing required before the command reaches the control loop.

Remote inference introduces more than a fixed delay. By the time an action chunk reaches the robot, it was generated from observations that are already stale, and the amount of staleness changes from one cycle to the next. Buffering, local stabilization, and real-time chunking can smooth the resulting discontinuities [X], but only by trading reactivity for stability. They cannot recover changes in the world that happened after the observation was captured.

Wireless reliability creates an additional bottleneck. Wi-Fi can exhibit substantial jitter, intermittent packet loss, and abrupt degradation as the robot moves through a building. A system that performs well near an access point may fail when the robot enters a basement, passes through a doorway, or encounters network congestion. During evaluations of Sunday Robotics’ autonomous ACT-2 policy in real homes, unpredictable Wi-Fi latency and network jitter reportedly emerged as major bottlenecks and failure modes, motivating a shift toward local, edge-compute control [X]. An autonomous robot cannot treat continuous datacenter connectivity as a dependable part of its control loop.

2. At 40 watts, power is the wall — not memory bandwidth#

The datacenter and the edge are solving almost opposite engineering problems. Much of modern datacenter accelerator design is organized around the memory-bandwidth wall: compute has grown faster than memory bandwidth, so HBM, larger caches, batching, operator fusion, and increasingly aggressive memory packaging are used to keep large compute arrays fed. A robot head operates at a very different point. We estimated the workload by counting: the model architecture fixes the operations per chunk (~7×10¹⁴, counted operator by operator), and the only data that must move off-chip — 7.0 GB of weights and a 7.7 GB context window, far too large for any on-die memory, each read once per step — fixes the bytes, about 44 GB per chunk. The ratio is roughly 16,000 FLOPs per byte fetched [S], while Jetson Thor’s published dense FP4 peak of 1,035 TFLOP/s and 273 GB/s of memory bandwidth give a nominal ridge near 3,800 FLOPs per byte [X: NVIDIA Jetson Thor Series Modules datasheet]. On paper, the workload therefore sits about 4× to the compute-bound side of the roofline. Real GPU traffic can erode that margin, which we measure separately below, but the basic implication is important: adding more external memory bandwidth is not the primary optimization available to us. At batch one and roughly 40 W, the scarce resource is the energy required to sustain useful computation.

We express that with independent lower bounds on chunk time:

t_chunk ≥ max( t_compute, t_memory, t_comm, t_energy ), t_energy = E_chunk / P_dynamic

These are lower bounds, not a schedule — sequencing and overlap live in the cycle model — and the energy bound dominates every other by more than an order of magnitude in our modeled configurations [S]; consistent with that, our anchor GPU sat pinned at its 600 W limit in every measured run [M]. An energy-limited device’s throughput is P / E_chunk, so at equal power, speedup is exactly the energy ratio:

S = η · (P_dut / P_base) = η at the same 40 W, η ≡ E_base / E_dut

Fewer joules per trajectory is proportionally more trajectories per second — the question is whether specialization can materially reduce those joules.

Why a purpose-built chip can beat a GPU at 40 W

Large architectural gains from specialization are real: TPUv4 achieved 1.6–3.2× higher performance per watt than A100 on measured workloads at the same process node [X: ISCA 2023]. The opportunity is not better multipliers. Modern GPUs already have highly optimized low-precision tensor cores; our anchor workload reaches 94.7 % of peak GEMM efficiency [M], FlashAttention is standard, and Blackwell supports FP8 and FP4 natively. The opportunity is in the overhead required to remain programmable.

Hameed et al. found that only about 6 % of a general-purpose processor’s energy reached the functional units, with much of the rest spent on clocking, caches, register files, control, and data movement [X: ISCA 2010]. These numbers do not describe Blackwell directly, but they show where specialization can help. Batch-one world-model inference has a largely fixed graph, tensor shapes, operator order, and memory-access pattern. Our workload launches roughly 1,200 GPU kernels per trajectory chunk [S]. An RPU can encode that schedule directly, stream weights predictably, keep intermediates local, and tailor the memory hierarchy to the workload.

The byte side shows the same overhead. WAN-class video models measure memory-bandwidth-bound even on a B200 [F], despite the on-paper margin above: a conventional unfused attention implementation writes roughly 25× that minimum byte count [S], and batch-one inference refetches the weights for every request.

The gain cannot come from matrix multiplication alone. Projections account for about 37 % of modeled arithmetic energy, so even eliminating their energy entirely would cap the gain at

1 / (1 − 0.37) ≈ 1.59×

Attention consumes roughly 62 % despite representing only 40.7 % of FLOPs [S]. The full gain therefore requires a cheaper end-to-end datapath: less register and shared-memory traffic, greater locality, deeper fusion, and fewer utilization losses. We do not count FlashAttention as an advantage; GPUs already use it. The hardware advantage is making the fused computation itself the datapath rather than executing it through a sequence of programmable kernels.

Our measurements also found that reducing GPU frequency from 3.1 to 1.0 GHz did not materially improve energy per FLOP [M]. The hypothesis is therefore simple: not radically better arithmetic, but much less energy spent on scheduling, movement, and programmability the workload does not need. TPUv4 establishes the scale of what specialization can recover; the simulator, cycle model, and physical-design gates determine how much of that advantage survives real implementation costs.

First-silicon specs [T]

  • 1.9 M FP4 MACs at ~1.05 GHz (4 PF dense) in 128×128 weight-streaming systolic tiles; the model widths divide the array exactly (5120 = 8 × 640, 13,824 = 8 × 1728).
  • FP8 multiply into FP32 accumulators in dedicated SRAM beneath the array — TPUv1’s narrow-multiply, wide-accumulate pattern [X].
  • 256-bit LPDDR5X interface at 307 GB/s, at least 16 GB; the prefetcher is a counter, because the chip walks the same ~44 GB address sequence every chunk.
  • ~90 MB SRAM for stream buffers and the activation spine — no resident weights.
  • KV ring in DRAM, advanced by pointer at chunk end; both guidance branches share each fetched weight and context block.
  • Per-bank DRAM refresh in known idle windows — left unmanaged, hot-head refresh costs 14–28 % of effective bandwidth and adds tail latency [S].
  • No operating system, zero instruction fetch, no kernels. A GPU launches ~1,200 kernels per chunk; the RPU executes one fixed hardware schedule. TPUv1 showed what this predictability is worth under a latency bound: 80 % of peak at 7 ms p99 versus 37 % for a same-generation K80 [X: ISCA 2017].
  • Schedule ROM with one-, two-, and three-step modes, generated offline by the calibrated simulator with a worst-case deadline certificate; weights, scales, token counts, and schedules load from an image — tile geometry and number formats stay fixed in the mask.
  • ~2 % of the datapath programmable for flow-ODE or CEM-style planning; a JEPA-family workload costs 1.9 % of DreamZero-path performance per watt, and both stream ~7 GB, so one memory tier serves both [S].
  • No further conventional-architecture multiplier. Grids of small cores, reconfigurable fabrics, and exposed datapaths preserve more flexibility but pay for it in measured efficiency [S].

Model-resident specs [T]

  • Memory stacked directly on compute cuts each weight read from ~40 to ~8 pJ/byte and moves the roofline that otherwise caps compute gains near 2×.
  • Bound rises to ~20–31×, or 29–44× with gated circuit techniques.
  • Three questions gate the bet: does the stack survive a fanless head, can we buy the capacity, and do hybrid bonds tolerate vibration?
  • Deepens our commitment to transformers — so it stays out of first silicon.

Codesign specs [T]

  • The ceiling is arithmetic, not a design claim: put every joule of the 40 W into FP4 multiply-accumulate at its floor (~0.0038 pJ/FLOP) against Thor’s achieved 0.13–0.18, and you get 35–47× — the most any FP4 chip can win at equal power.
  • Past that, gains must come from the model, not the silicon.

The memory-bandwidth margin

Memory bandwidth is the tightest specification and the part of the model we trust least because we have not yet calibrated against a memory-bound workload. The current design sits only about 1.5× on the compute side of the roofline crossover, and hot-token refresh consumes part of that margin. A moderate traffic-accounting error could therefore make the chip memory-bound [S].

We have several conventional ways to recover the margin. Lossless weight-stream compression cuts demand to 62–83 %, while a 512-bit LPDDR5X interface provides roughly 614 GB/s; Apple already ships 546 GB/s on-package [X]. Even a conservative FP8 configuration streaming ~15.5 GB per step remains below modeled compute time [S].

We can also trade MAC area for memory channels, and short-reach on-package memory could lower cost per bit several-fold [S]. Gate 1 will tell us how much bandwidth the real workload needs from measured B200 profiling, and we will size the memory system from that result [F].

Still open

Seven questions can still change the architecture:

  1. Attention datapath. We need to measure the full FP8 streaming path — online softmax, exponential, accumulation, and local storage. If attention keeps functional-unit utilization below the energy fraction required for η, the architecture fails.
  2. Dataflow. We still need a pass-by-pass comparison of weight-stationary, output-stationary, and broadcast-tree mappings on the exact model shapes. A stall-free conveyor weakens the usual case for output-stationary execution, and published work does not resolve this for chunk diffusion.
  3. Energy ledger. We will account for every millijoule at tile level. If mapped η falls below 2.2, or functional units receive less than 35 % of total energy, we stop before tapeout.
  4. Sub-Vmin operation. Etched reports half-voltage math blocks in A0 silicon, and near-threshold work supports split voltage domains and 8T SRAM. Razor-style recovery is less convincing: its often-cited ~47 % gain is relative to signoff margin rather than energy per inference, published silicon loses efficiency below the voltage floor, and replay makes latency variable. Production processors have instead used monitor circuits to adjust voltage per tile. Measured accelerators including memory and interconnect show about 3.3× rather than the ~9× often reported in idealized simulations. Once we include fixed-voltage memory, the system-level gain may fall to only ~20 %. We will prove it on a test tile or leave it out of η.
  5. Robot memory engine. Persistent latent state, episodic retrieval, fast-weight layers, and bounded context may eventually deserve hardware support. A write-capable weight path breaks the current read-only conveyor assumption, so we must scope it before RTL freezes that choice.
  6. Compiler. It starts as a scheduling tool and can grow into a PyTorch→StableHLO→MLIR backend. The calibrated simulator already supplies its cost model; the long-term software advantage comes from coupling compilation, scheduling, and memory design.
  7. Whether HBM returns. We originally rejected HBM on power grounds and designed around LPDDR. If specialization frees enough power, HBM may become practical and eliminate the bandwidth question. We made the original decision before building the calibrated model, so we need to test it again.

4. Build the simulator before the silicon#

We cannot fabricate the target hardware yet, so we use the simulator to kill weak ideas before they reach silicon. We test it first against hardware we can measure.

Simulating Blackwell’s efficiency

We calibrated it against an RTX PRO 6000 Blackwell workstation using the real workload, tensor shapes, and one-, two-, and three-step schedules. We measured execution time directly and sampled board power with nvidia-smi; the simulator predicted each run blind [M]. It matched execution time within 0.5–3.9 % and energy within 0.8–2.5 % [M]. Every claim on this site is a comparison against Thor, and this calibration pins the Thor side of it: Thor is a Blackwell-generation GPU, so the model is predicting the thing it was calibrated on. It also validates the quantities both architectures share — operation counts, byte counts, and the relationship between energy and speed.

Measurement has already killed two attractive assumptions [M]. First, running wider at lower clock saves essentially nothing: after subtracting idle power, energy per FLOP remains flat from 1.0 to 3.1 GHz, so a real gain requires custom low-voltage or sub-Vmin circuitry, not simply lowering frequency. Second, precision scaling under-delivers: FP16→FP8 yielded 1.77–1.82× rather than 2×, so we model FP16→FP4 at 2.6–3.4×, not the ~10× suggested by ideal arithmetic ratios.

Simulating our chip’s efficiency

A GPU cannot tell us how much energy a systolic array will consume, so we estimate RPU energy independently, with a cycle-level model of the RPU dataflow and circuit analysis [T]. It comes down to three numbers, each modeled today as a range rather than a point estimate, and each replaced by a gate-1 measurement: the energy of one FP4 multiply-accumulate (0.0038–0.0057 pJ/FLOP — pinned by post-layout synthesis on a real process kit), the share of the module’s energy that reaches the arithmetic (0.15–0.35 — pinned by the per-component energy ledger from those same runs), and how much of the silicon’s ceiling the compiler extracts (0.55–0.95 — pinned by a scaled FPGA running real schedules). If those measurements break the efficiency case, we stop.

5. Win the transition to edge inference#

Our reference workload is the Wan2.1-I2V-14B / DreamZero class of video-action diffusion transformer: roughly 14 billion parameters, 40 layers, 40 attention heads, hidden width 5,120, and FFN width 13,824. Each latent frame contributes about 1,560 tokens. A rolling two-second context contains roughly 18,720 tokens, with another 1,560–3,120 added per update. The system must execute one to three denoising steps, often across a guidance pair, and return an action trajectory within a 200 ms control cycle.

That workload does not yet fit within the power, memory, and thermal envelope of a humanoid robot head. Four-bit weights occupy about 7.0 GB and the FP8 KV window another 7.7 GB, both streamed from external memory on every step. Self-attention contributes about 40.7 % of FLOPs, FFN 30.0 %, QKV projection 16.7 %, cross-attention 7.0 %, and output projection 5.6 % [S]. With guidance fan-out, one step reads about 14.8 GB of model state before sharing the same weights across both branches.

Full edge inference will require both better models and better hardware. We do not need to know exactly where those curves meet. We need to stay ahead of general-purpose robotics platforms as they converge, using that lead for higher refresh rates, larger deployable models, lower power, and progressively more local inference.

Our first-product target is 5× [T] — deliberately in the favorable half of the modeled range. At equal power, the central design point evaluates at 2.9× over Thor with a first-generation compiler and exceeds 4× as the compiler matures [S]; reaching 5× requires the compiler work landing early and the physical-design measurements breaking our way. We compare against Thor’s dense-compute rating because the workload uses no sparsity. We set the minimum useful advantage at 2×.

Under the most adverse combination of assumptions, the advantage falls to 1.9×. If every input lands favorably, it reaches 15.7×, or 22× if gated low-voltage techniques also work. We treat those upper numbers as ceilings, not forecasts.

The larger opportunity appears once the model architecture stabilizes. We can then co-design model and accelerator: fix dataflows, specialize memory movement, fuse repeated operations, remove unnecessary precision and programmability, and eventually harden stable network structure directly into silicon.

The end state is not simply a faster processor running a world model. It is an ASIC whose architecture is the world model.

That is not the first product. First, we need to beat Thor while retaining enough flexibility to follow a model architecture that is still changing.
