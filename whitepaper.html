Whitepaper — July 2026

A measured case for etching world-model inference into a robot’s head

The ChatGPT moment for robotics requires world models to run on the robot.

Source tags: [M] measured ourselves · [S] computed by our simulator · [F] measured in production at a robotics company · [X] published elsewhere · [T] a target, not yet reached.

Executive summary

Humanoid robotics is converging on video-action diffusion transformers that predict several seconds of motion and must replan fast enough to achieve human-like reaction latency. Today’s leading models are still too large to run inside a robot head, forcing teams to rely on kilowatt-class datacenter GPUs and continuous network connections. That puts latency, Wi-Fi jitter, and connectivity failures directly inside the robot’s control loop [F].

Our immediate goal is to build a specialized accelerator that runs these workloads at least 2× faster than NVIDIA Thor at the same 40-watt power budget, with a design target closer to 3× [S]. That advantage creates an immediate competitive edge: more compelling robot demos, larger deployable models, lower inference costs, and more of the control loop running locally.

Full edge inference will require model efficiency and hardware efficiency to improve together. As world models become smaller through distillation, quantization, token reduction, caching, and better architectures, we intend to remain several steps ahead of general-purpose platforms at every stage of that transition. That near-term lead wins deployments and gives us the workload knowledge, software stack, and customer relationships needed for the larger opportunity. When the model architecture finally stabilizes, we will be positioned to co-design the model and chip as a single system — creating an accelerator that NVIDIA’s general-purpose architecture cannot match.

1. The workload arrived before the chip did

Leading humanoid robotics programs are converging on a common architecture: a video-action diffusion transformer predicts a multi-second future trajectory and replans several times per second [X: DreamZero]. The resulting trajectory is streamed to the robot and used as a reference by its lower-level control stack. At the model scales and inference rates required for capable general-purpose behavior, these systems still cannot run on the edge. As a result, teams have been forced to build extraordinary infrastructure around remote inference — including low-latency video transport, datacenter accelerators, trajectory streaming, delay compensation, and local fallback control.

The critical system-level metric is reaction latency: the elapsed time between capturing an observation and executing the corresponding action. Human visuomotor reaction time is roughly 200 milliseconds, and delays substantially beyond this range are readily perceived as sluggishness or hesitation. For a robot, this “glass-to-action” latency includes every stage of the pipeline: sensor capture and onboard IPC or bus transfers; transmission of camera frames and proprioceptive state to the datacenter; the model’s inference loop; transmission of the generated trajectory back to the robot; and the final onboard processing required before the command reaches the control loop.

Remote inference introduces more than a fixed delay. Network latency and, more importantly, latency variation can produce irregular motion unless the system employs effective buffering, local stabilization, and delay compensation. Every robot exhibits some tracking error between commanded and realized motion, but remote inference adds a second source of error: each action chunk may be generated from observations and action history that are already stale — and stale by a different amount on every cycle — by the time the command is executed.

During that delay, both the robot and its environment continue to evolve. The model’s predicted rollout can therefore diverge from the physical state in which the trajectory is ultimately applied. In action-chunked or autoregressive systems, these inconsistencies can propagate across successive predictions, creating discontinuities, drift, or even instability in ways that average latency alone does not reveal.

Techniques such as real-time chunking attempt to smooth transitions between successive trajectories through trajectory inpainting or temporal blending, while the downstream controller can damp abrupt changes before they reach the actuators [X]. These mechanisms can reduce visible transients, but they cannot reconstruct changes in the world that occurred after the original observations were captured. They also introduce a fundamental tradeoff between smoothness and reactivity: stronger blending, buffering, and damping make motion more stable, but reduce the robot’s ability to respond quickly to new information.

Wireless reliability creates an additional bottleneck. Wi-Fi can exhibit substantial jitter, intermittent packet loss, and abrupt degradation as the robot moves through a building. A system that performs well near an access point may fail when the robot enters a basement, passes through a doorway, or encounters network congestion. During evaluations of Sunday Robotics’ autonomous ACT-2 policy in real homes, unpredictable Wi-Fi latency and network jitter reportedly emerged as major bottlenecks and failure modes, motivating a shift toward local, edge-compute control [X]. The broader lesson is that a robot intended to operate autonomously in unstructured environments cannot treat continuous datacenter connectivity as a dependable part of its control loop.

2. Win the transition to edge inference

The workload we must serve today is already extreme. Our reference point is the Wan2.1-I2V-14B / DreamZero class of video-action diffusion transformer: roughly 14 billion parameters, 40 layers, 40 attention heads, a hidden width of 5,120, and an FFN width of 13,824. Each latent frame contributes approximately 1,560 tokens, with a rolling two-second context of roughly 18,720 tokens and another 1,560–3,120 tokens introduced on each update. The system must execute one to three denoising steps, often across a guidance pair, and return a new action trajectory within a 200 ms control-cycle budget.

That workload cannot yet run inside the power, memory, and thermal envelope of a humanoid robot head. Four-bit weights alone occupy approximately 7.0 GB, while the FP8 KV window contributes another 7.7 GB. Both must be streamed from external memory on every step. The computation is similarly demanding: self-attention accounts for approximately 40.7 % of the FLOPs, the FFN 30.0 %, QKV projection 16.7 %, cross-attention 7.0 %, and output projection 5.6 % [S]. (These figures were corrected in August 2026: the cross-attention term had charged its key and value projections over the full token chunk rather than the much shorter text sequence. A second, independently written cycle-level model now reproduces the split exactly.) With guidance fan-out, a single step reads approximately 14.8 GB of model state before feeding both branches from the same weights.

The eventual objective is to run this entire control workload locally. Reaching that point requires two trends to converge. First, the models must become dramatically smaller and more efficient through better architectures, distillation, quantization, caching, and reduced-step generation. Second, edge accelerators must continue improving in compute density, memory bandwidth, and energy efficiency — the continuation of the hardware scaling trend often described as Jensen’s law. We believe these curves will meet. Our goal is to build the hardware platform positioned to capture that moment when they do.

But we do not need to wait for full edge autonomy to build a valuable company. Our near-term objective is more concrete: maintain a substantial performance multiple over general-purpose robotics platforms such as NVIDIA Thor on the workloads that matter for world-model inference. That advantage can take the form of higher trajectory refresh rates, larger deployable models, lower power, lower latency, or some combination of all four. Even before the entire model fits inside the robot, a purpose-built accelerator can reduce datacenter requirements, improve remote-inference economics, enable more capable onboard fallbacks, and move progressively larger portions of the control loop onto the edge. Sustaining that performance lead is the immediate engineering target and the basis on which we can win the market. The simulation page puts numbers on that lead: at the same power, our current design points put the RPU module at roughly 2.9× the Thor module’s speed with a first-generation compiler, rising past 4× as the compiler matures — figures that moved upward in August 2026 when we corrected the comparison to Thor’s dense-compute rating, the only one our no-sparsity workload can use. Our frozen success bars (2× solidly, in every mode) are unchanged and sit well below these estimates. The full published range runs from 1.9× if every uncertainty breaks against us to a ceiling of 15.7× with every input at its favorable end — 22× if gated low-voltage circuit techniques prove out. The ceiling is not a forecast; it is the north star the design steers toward. The full ladder, with each number’s standing:

The long-term opportunity is larger. Once the model architecture stabilizes, the distinction between model design and chip design begins to disappear. We can co-design the network and the accelerator together: fixing dataflows, specializing memory movement, fusing repeated operations, eliminating unnecessary precision and programmability, and ultimately etching the structure of the neural network into the hardware itself. The result would not merely be a faster processor running a world model. It would be an ASIC whose architecture is the world model — an accelerator that a general-purpose platform cannot economically match.

That is the destination, not the first product. The immediate task is to build a practical accelerator that stays several steps ahead of Thor while preserving enough architectural flexibility to follow a rapidly evolving model layer. By winning the intermediate stages of the transition, we earn the deployments, workload knowledge, compiler stack, and silicon experience required to build the fully co-designed system when the model architecture is ready.

3. At 40 watts, energy sets the clock

A robot head can push roughly 40 W out through the neck [S]. Chunk time is whichever of four bounds is worst:

t = max( t_compute, t_memory, t_comm, E_chunk / (TDP · (1 − f_static)) )

On Thor and on our design alike, the fourth one wins by an order of magnitude [S]. So adding peak FLOPS without reducing joules per FLOP does nothing whatsoever.

When two chips are both energy-limited, throughput cancels out of the comparison entirely and you are left with:

S = η · (TDP_dut / TDP_base)

η is how much less energy our datapath spends per FLOP than the GPU we calibrated against [S]. We give our module the same 40 W as the Thor module, so the TDP ratio is one and S = η exactly: at η = 1 the two modules tie, and every point of efficiency is a point of speed. The whole product question is that one number.

4. Build the simulator before the silicon

We cannot fabricate the target hardware yet, so our first job is to build the most credible simulation environment possible. The simulator gives us a way to explore architectures, reject weak ideas, and quantify tradeoffs before committing millions of dollars to silicon. But a simulator is only useful if it can reproduce hardware that already exists.

We began by calibrating it against an RTX PRO 6000 Blackwell workstation. We ran the actual target workload using the same tensor geometries and one-, two-, and three-step denoising configurations assumed elsewhere in this design. For each trajectory chunk, we measured execution time directly and sampled board power through nvidia-smi [M]. We then asked the simulator to predict those same runs without access to the measured results.

Across the tested configurations, the simulator came within 0.5–3.9 % on execution time and 0.8–2.5 % on energy [M]. We are also working with experts in accelerator architecture, physical design, memory systems, compilers, and robotics inference to challenge the simulator’s assumptions and validate the ideas derived from it. Their role is not merely to confirm the design, but to identify where simplified models omit real implementation constraints: routing congestion, utilization losses, synchronization overhead, power delivery, thermal limits, memory-controller behavior, and the cost of preserving programmability. The simulator is therefore not a substitute for domain expertise; it is a shared quantitative framework through which those experts can test and refine the architecture.

The value of this process is already visible. Direct measurement has forced us to abandon two initially attractive ideas [M]:

  1. Running wider at a lower clock does not automatically save energy. We expected reduced frequency and voltage to improve energy efficiency. On the measured GPU, however, energy per FLOP remains approximately flat from 1.0 to 3.1 GHz after subtracting idle power. A meaningful gain would require custom low-voltage or sub-Vmin circuit design; it cannot be obtained simply by selecting a lower operating point on existing silicon.
  2. Precision scaling delivers less than ideal arithmetic ratios suggest. Moving from FP16 to FP8 produced a measured speedup of 1.77–1.82×, rather than the theoretical 2×. We therefore model FP16-to-FP4 improvement using a measured prior of 2.6–3.4×, replacing the roughly 10× gain assumed in an earlier version of the design. That correction alone increased our absolute energy estimates by approximately threefold.

5. Where the multiple comes from

We pushed the uncertainty through Monte Carlo — token count, how far the schedule compresses, the precision prior — and the number that falls out is η* ≈ 2.02 for a median 2× against Thor at the same power. The back-of-envelope agrees: 2 × 40/40 = 2 [S]. The bar itself is 2×, frozen in a machine-readable contract before design-space exploration; because a median is a coin flip we verify it at the 5th percentile in every mode, which in the current model corresponds to η ≥ 2.15, and the target of 3 survives even if we hand Thor 50 W [S]. Measured against Thor’s full 130 W bench rating the design lands at 0.66–0.92×. The RPU is a head part, and we would rather say that than quote the flattering comparison [S].

Is that reachable? TPUv4 got 1.6–3.2× performance per watt over an A100 on the same process node, on measured power [X: ISCA 2023]. Our bar sits in the middle of that range and our target at the top of it. Neither asks for anything nobody has built.

The more useful guidance is older. Hameed and colleagues took a general-purpose chip apart and found only 5.8 % of its energy reaching the functional units — the transistors that actually do arithmetic. Fusing custom datapaths pushes that toward 35 %, and what closes the remaining processor-to-ASIC gap is custom storage wired directly to those units. Not removing instructions, and not wider SIMD [X: ISCA 2010]. Tensor cores already did the fused-matmul part, so our headroom is everything around the multiplier: pipeline and clocking (22 % of their ledger), caches (19 %), register files (10 %), control (10 %).

Attention is where this gets awkward, though not for the reason we first gave. Because attention runs at FP8 while the linear layers run at FP4, roughly 62 % of the arithmetic energy is attention and 37 % is matmul and projections [S]. (Corrected August 2026, prompted by an external reviewer: an earlier version of this sentence added “under 1 % is moving bytes” — a fossil of the picojoule-per-bit-for-per-byte unit error we fixed elsewhere that month. Byte movement is a separate, system-level category, not part of this arithmetic split: roughly 4–5 % of chunk energy under the launch accounting, rising to the 20–33 % quoted in the memory section as the arithmetic improves — which is exactly why the 3D-memory lever is priced there and not here.) The cause of the split is precision, not data movement: self-attention is 40.7 % of the arithmetic but 73.3 % of the arithmetic energy, because cost scales with the square of operand width. Set matmul energy to exactly zero and the speedup still caps at 1.59×, so there is no version of this chip that wins on matrix multiply alone.

We corrected this section in August 2026, and the correction is unflattering. We had presented fused streaming attention as an advantage stacked on top of a TPU-style control path. That was double-counting. FlashAttention has been the PyTorch default since 2.0 and our own measured baseline used it; Blackwell has native FP8 and native FP4. A GPU running this workload already fuses its attention and already has both formats. What is left is implementation efficiency rather than a new algorithm: a GPU still alternates between tensor cores for the two matrix multiplies and vector units for the exponential and the softmax, losing utilization at every handoff, and keys and values still travel through shared memory and the register file. Those are real, and they are smaller than what we claimed.

The result is a simpler and more testable thesis. There is no separate attention multiplier to add to a control-path advantage. Everything reduces to one ratio: the share of energy each design delivers to its multipliers. The attention datapath is where that share has to be earned, competing against every other decision for the same budget, rather than a bonus on top.

6. What we would build

The RPU: a head-resident inference engine, sized to the shapes above. Targets unless tagged otherwise [T]:

  • About 1.9 M FP4 multiply-accumulate units at ~1.05 GHz (4 PF dense) in 128×128 weight-streaming systolic tiles. The model's widths divide the array exactly: 5120 = 8 × 640, and 13 824 = 8 × 1728.
  • FP8 multiply into FP32 accumulators held in dedicated SRAM beneath the array — the narrow-multiply, wide-accumulate pattern from TPUv1 [X].
  • A 256-bit LPDDR5X interface at 307 GB/s and at least 16 GB. The prefetcher is a counter, because the chip reads the identical ~44 GB address sequence every single chunk. About 90 MB of on-die SRAM holds stream buffers and the activation spine, and never a resident set of weights.
  • A KV ring that lives in DRAM and advances by pointer at chunk end. Weights and context are fetched once per step and fanned out to both guidance branches in silicon.
  • Refresh gets its own slot. The sequencer issues per-bank DRAM refresh in idle windows it already knows about. Left alone, hot-head refresh derating costs 14–28 % of effective bandwidth and adds tail latency; scheduling it removes the interference by construction [S].
  • No operating system, and no instructions. The same chunk costs about 1 200 kernel launches on a GPU and zero in-band instructions here. Being predictable is what makes it fast, not a tax on speed: under a 7 ms p99 bound the original TPU held 80 % of peak while a same-generation K80 fell to 37 % [X: ISCA 2017]. Our 200 ms loop is tighter than that benchmark, not looser.
  • A schedule ROM with 1-, 2- and 3-step modes, produced offline by a tool that reuses the calibrated simulator as its cost model and emits a worst-case deadline certificate per mode. Weights, scales, token counts and schedules load from an image; tile geometry and number formats are fixed in the mask.
  • A small programmable block, about 2 % of the datapath, for flow-ODE or CEM-style planning. Supporting a JEPA-family workload costs 1.9 % of DreamZero-path performance per watt — both families stream 7 GB of weights, so no memory tier separates them [S].

The requirement is three denoising steps with guidance. At η = 2.15 — the 5th-percentile-every-mode criterion for the 2× bar — against Thor at the same 40 W [S], the RPU lands a 2.13× win. At the design target of 3 the median reaches 2.95×, and in its highest-quality planning mode the chip finishes a two-second imagined trajectory in 4.5 s where Thor needs 13 [S]. The relative bar clears.

We do not clear an absolute 200 ms — the model puts that at η ≈ 12, which is outside every evidence band we have. Closing that gap is not a silicon problem alone, and the next section is what we would do about it.

Heat removal is part of the design

Every watt the neck can move multiplies speed directly, so we treat the thermal path as a component of the chip rather than packaging. The etched schedule gives the package a power trace known at compile time: the guardband shrinks to manufacturing variation, and no governor throttles mid-chunk. The die spreads its 40 watts across roughly 450 mm², under 9 W/cm² with no hotspots, which is what keeps the head fanless [T].

Cooling the robot itself provides — cold plates under 3 mm are already mainstream in humanoids, and heat pipes can move rejection into a torso radiator — raises the ceiling for any chip equally, so we score it symmetrically and claim none of it as η [S]. What survives asymmetrically: the guardband, the flat power density, and shipping the reference thermal design with the part. Die-embedded microfluidic cooling, which only a chip designer can build in, is the gated option that would make 3D-stacked DRAM thermally survivable [X].

7. The model has to meet us halfway

Better hardware alone will not put today's largest world-action models inside a robot head. The model has to become smaller, faster and easier to execute at the same time. Two different families of lever do that work: the ones the chip exploits at runtime, inside a chunk, and the ones the model team applies at training time, before the weights ever ship. All of these techniques already exist in the literature. The open question is not whether they exist, but how aggressively they can be combined without degrading closed-loop behaviour.

What the chip exploits at runtime

We went through the workload optimizations one at a time and checked each against its own literature rather than its abstract. Below is what each one actually buys, as a multiplier on chunk energy. These are the multipliers on the three-step schedule the requirement assumes [T].

Cross-chunk reuse leads at 1.3–2.0× in Quality mode and 1.0–1.45× in Deadline against a certified floor: consecutive chunks re-diffuse world state that mostly overlaps, so the overlap gets computed once instead of every time. WorldCache reports 2.3× at 99.4 % quality on a video world model and Chorus 1.45× on 4-step distilled models [X]. Sliding-tile sparse attention, which attends within local tiles instead of sweeping the whole 18 720-token window every step, holds 1.15–1.45× in either mode. Token merging collapses visually redundant tokens before they reach the transformer, worth 1.1–1.3×. Cheaper arithmetic in the attention normalization adds a percent or two. Per-step feature caching, the one everybody suggests first, buys us nothing: it needs many steps to cache across and we run one to three.

Compose those, discount for the work they duplicate, and the range is roughly 1.7–3.8× [T]. Two caveats belong with that number rather than after it. Every lever is gated on task success: one that saves energy and drops the robot is not a lever. And the range is composed with 2:4 sparsity and FP4 KV switched on, which the constraint below holds in reserve. Read it as the ceiling with everything enabled, not the figure to plan against.

Cross-chunk reuse is the one we would build first. It is the largest single multiplier, and it survives few-step schedules where per-step caching does not. No language-model accelerator has any reason to build it, which leaves it to us. The published numbers are encouraging: WorldCache reports 2.3× at 99.4 % quality on a video world model, and Chorus reports 1.45× on 4-step distilled models [X].

There is a catch worth stating plainly. The schedule still has to reserve the worst-case slot, so reuse saves energy without making the deadline proof any stronger. It only counts toward the deadline if training can guarantee a floor on how much overlap there will be — and train-time chunking is the tool that could train that floor in [F].

The state of the art in model compression

Here is the toolbox as the field currently holds it, ordered by what each part saves us.

Quantization. FP8 and carefully calibrated INT8 already run close to lossless on many workloads. Reaching INT4 or FP4 reliably takes quantization-aware training, distillation, or both (QVGen, NVIDIA's NVFP4 work) [X]. Precision does not have to be uniform either: the sensitive dynamics or prediction core can stay at higher precision while quantization-tolerant layers drop lower. For transformer world models, quantizing the KV cache and the intermediate activations can matter as much as quantizing weights, because long rollouts spend as much memory on state as on parameters. Our own design point already assumes FP8 activations and FP4 linear weights, so it presumes a working QAT recipe [S].

Fewer denoising steps. For diffusion and flow-based video-action models this is the dominant lever. Consistency and trajectory distillation (LCM, FRMD), progressive distillation, distribution matching (DMD, DMD2), adversarial distillation (LADD) and self-distilled flow maps (One-Step Flow Policy) compress a long sampling trajectory into one to four steps [X]. Modality-aware methods go further by treating video and action differently during training rather than forcing both through the same noise schedule (Flash-WAM). Our 16→3 result already banks part of this [F].

A smaller network. A student can learn to reproduce a larger teacher's latent predictions, imagined rollouts or actions. For control, the strongest versions train the student on states reached under its own behaviour rather than only copying teacher trajectories (ActDistill, on-policy distillation) [X] — small errors that look harmless in an offline dataset compound once the student is in the loop. Quantization-aware distillation does both at once: the student learns to stay accurate while running at low precision from the start. This route is available to us and untested at our scale; in the action-only setting students already match or beat their teachers.

Changing what the model represents. Pixel reconstruction is expensive and often unnecessary for control. Latent-space models predict in a compressed representation and decode only when needed; reconstruction-free designs drop the decoder entirely and work over features from a frozen perception model (MuDreamer, DINO-WM). Discrete or tokenized latents (DreamerV2/V3, IRIS, TWM), stronger spatial and temporal compression and fewer tokens per frame all attack sequence length directly. Two of these we already bank, since our fork drops the pixel decoder and predicts in latent space; the token-count reductions are still on the table, and they land on the two inputs our sensitivity ranking puts on top. These are not implementation details: they change the geometry of the workload the chip has to execute.

Architecture and reuse. State-space and Mamba-style models, linear or masked attention, hybrid backbones, low-rank factorization, parameter sharing and smaller purpose-built DiTs each cut the cost of long-horizon prediction. Frozen foundation encoders (DINO, SAM, VAE latents) supply perception without every world model carrying its own visual stack. Structured pruning removes whole heads, channels or layers in a form hardware can actually exploit, and only the structured kind helps us, because our tiles are fixed in the mask; and caching reuses features, keys and values or activations across nearby frames and denoising steps.

The two families differ, and the word “distilled” hides it. In latent-planning systems (Dreamer, DINO-WM, TD-MPC) the opportunities are compact latent dynamics, smaller students, shorter contexts and distilled policies. In generative video models the dominant costs are token count, network size and repeated denoising, so step distillation and feature reuse outrank parameter count. Calling a model “distilled” is therefore incomplete: it may mean a smaller network, fewer sampling steps, or a policy distilled out of the world model. We are in the second family.

None of this is speculative. Quantization, step reduction, latent compression, caching, structured sparsity and student-teacher distillation are all established. The engineering opportunity is to combine them around the requirements of closed-loop robotics instead of optimizing each one independently for image quality or offline benchmarks.

Three things follow, and they matter more than the catalogue. First, we cannot count the baseline twice: latent-space modelling, the dropped decoder, the three-step schedule and mixed FP8/FP4 are already inside the numbers in Section 6, so the multipliers above sit on top of that baseline rather than beside it. Second, some model choices reach back into the chip. A state-space or linear-attention backbone would retire much of the attention energy this chip exists to make cheap, and token compression moves the exact parameters the array and the memory interface are sized around. Our whole thesis rests on the workload holding its shape (Section 2), so the model roadmap is a chip input, not a parallel track — gate 1 fixes that contract before RTL commits to a shape. Third, the constraint below bounds the quantization row.

One constraint bounds the whole section: the control policy must not depend on aggressive quantization — no INT2 weights, no FP4 KV, no 2:4 pruning [F]. Those stay last resorts, behind everything bit-exact and structural.

The headroom, and the long game

Everything above is public, which means none of it is a moat and all of it is available to Thor as well. Two asymmetries survive that. The chip realises the runtime levers at a cost a GPU cannot match, which is the first table. And on the training side, the action-only setting is close to saturated — students now match or beat their teachers — while joint video-action distillation stays open, because the two streams carry different signal-to-noise schedules and one consistency objective serves neither well. Per-modality treatment is the current frontier. That is our model-side bet, and it pairs with the certifiable reuse floor in Section 9.

There is real model research left. Better latent representations could keep the physical information control needs while throwing away most of the visual detail. Better distillation objectives could reach one or two steps without collapsing the diversity of possible futures. Joint video-action models could run different architectures, precisions and update rates for perception, prediction and action. A model designed for an accelerator from the beginning may look very different from a video generator adapted to robotics afterwards.

Our roadmap assumes progress on both fronts and depends on a breakthrough in neither. In the near term we design for the workload that exists and exploit what is already available, so the first product stands on its own. Over time, model compression and architectural improvement move more of the workload onto the robot. As the model architecture stabilises, the boundary between model optimization and chip optimization becomes artificial, and full co-design opens up: representation, numerical formats, dataflow, memory hierarchy and silicon chosen together.

The chip does not have to carry the whole burden, and neither does the model. Edge inference becomes practical when model efficiency and hardware efficiency meet in the middle. Our immediate objective is to stay ahead of general-purpose hardware while that convergence runs. Our long-term objective is to help decide the architecture it converges on, and then build the silicon around it.

8. The remaining memory-bandwidth risk

There is one important inconsistency in our current analysis. Profiling shows that WAN-class video models are limited by memory bandwidth on a B200 [F]. Our simulator, however, predicts that the same workload would be limited primarily by compute on the proposed accelerator. We think there are three reasons for the difference.

First, GPUs move much more intermediate data than a fully fused accelerator would. A conventional, unfused attention implementation can write roughly 2.3 GB of attention scores per layer — about 25 times the total data movement assumed by our fused pipeline. On a GPU, whether those intermediate values can be eliminated depends on kernel fusion, compiler behavior, and hardware utilization. On a purpose-built chip, the fused dataflow would be part of the architecture itself [S].

Second, batch-one inference is particularly inefficient for GPUs. Because the workload processes one control request at a time, there is little opportunity to reuse weights across a batch. The GPU must repeatedly fetch a large model for relatively little computation, which can move the workload from the compute-bound side of the roofline to the bandwidth-bound side.

Third, our simulator has not yet been calibrated against a workload that is known to be memory-bound. This is the least validated part of the model, and therefore the part we trust least. Adding detailed B200 profiling is a gate 1 requirement, not an optional refinement [F].

This matters because the current design does not have a large margin. It sits only about 1.5× on the compute-bound side of the roofline crossover, and more frequent refreshes of the most recent tokens consume much of that margin [S]. A moderate error in our bandwidth assumptions could therefore change the system from compute-bound to memory-bound.

If that happens, the available fixes are well understood. Lossless compression of the weight stream could reduce bandwidth demand to roughly 62–83 % of its current level, and hardware capable of decoding one weight per clock has already been demonstrated [X]. A 512-bit LPDDR5X interface can provide roughly 614 GB/s, while current Apple systems already deliver about 546 GB/s of on-package bandwidth. LPDDR6 should provide substantially more bandwidth per package. Longer term, processing-in-memory could perform some KV-cache operations inside the memory banks, while hybrid-bonded 3D DRAM could reduce the energy cost of moving each bit [X].

Even under a conservative configuration — FP8 weights and no aggressive quantization — the model would stream approximately 15.5 GB per denoising step. Our current estimate is that this still fits beneath the compute time when paired with a 512-bit LPDDR5X interface [S]. However, compute area and memory-interface area trade directly against one another. Adding more MAC units is not useful if the memory system cannot feed them, while adding unnecessary memory channels increases area, power, and packaging complexity.

For that reason, the final balance between compute and bandwidth will be set at gate 1, after we have calibrated the simulator against real B200 profiling. The worst case appears manageable: it would require a 512-bit memory interface and lossless weight compression, but not an unproven memory technology or aggressive quantization [F].

So the unresolved question is not whether a solution exists. We have practical options across the range of bandwidth requirements we currently consider plausible. The remaining uncertainty is which option the real workload actually needs. That is a measurement problem, and obtaining that measurement is one of the main objectives of gate 1.

9. What is still unsolved

Each of these is open, each one matters, and each comes with a way to tell whether you are making progress.

  1. The attention energy path. Design the streamer that makes 62 % of dynamic energy cheap: division-free online softmax, fused exponential-multiply, static maximum bounds, and the right split of dataflow across fabric passes. Section 5 is the argument that the thesis fails without this.
  2. The dataflow question. Weight-stationary against output-stationary against broadcast-tree, on our exact shapes, pass by pass. A stall-free conveyor removes output-stationary's usual advantage, and nobody has published this comparison for chunk diffusion.
  3. The energy ledger. Build the tile-level model and produce our own version of Hameed's Table 3: millijoules per chunk, per component. This one can kill the project cheaply — mapped η below 2.2, or functional units below 35 % of energy, and we stop before anyone spends tapeout money.
  4. The memory-bound anchor. Ingest real B200 profiling, identify the realized bandwidth-utilization coefficient, locate the crossover, and set the ratio of MACs to memory channels.
  5. Sub-Vmin operation. Etched claims half-voltage math blocks in A0 silicon, and the near-threshold literature offers split voltage domains and 8T SRAM. We had also counted on Razor-style margin recovery, quoted around 47 %; we have since dropped it. That figure measured recovery against signoff margins rather than energy per inference, and published silicon shows efficiency actually falling once you push below the voltage floor. It also recovers errors by re-running work, which makes latency variable — unacceptable when the whole point is a provable deadline. The technique that has genuinely shipped in production processors is a monitor circuit driving per-tile voltage adjustment, and that is what we now plan for. On sizing, we were also too generous: measured silicon for a real accelerator with memory and interconnect gives about 3.3×, against roughly 9× for the idealised simulations usually cited. Once the memory interface is included, which runs at a fixed voltage and cannot be scaled at all, this is worth perhaps 20 % at the system level. Prove it on a test tile or leave it out of η permanently.
  6. A certifiable reuse floor. Co-design train-time chunking with the scheduler so cross-chunk reuse carries a trained, provable minimum. That converts a heuristic into deadline credit.
  7. The robot memory engine. Persistent latent state, episodic retrieval, fast-weight layers, bounded context — in hardware. A write-capable weight path breaks the read-only conveyor assumption, so this has to be scoped before RTL freezes that assumption in.
  8. The compiler. A scheduling tool today; a PyTorch→StableHLO→MLIR backend as the platform grows. The moat is compiler plus scheduling plus memory hierarchy, and the cost model for it already exists as the calibrated simulator.
  9. Whether HBM comes back. We ruled it out early on power grounds and built the entire memory argument around LPDDR. If the etched datapath frees enough of the budget, HBM may be affordable after all — and it would retire the bandwidth question outright. That assumption predates the calibrated model and has never been re-tested against it.
  10. How far the schedule distils. Getting to one step at reduced token count with task success intact is unproven. It is the lever that moves absolute latency rather than the ratio, so it decides whether 5 Hz is ever reachable.

9b. Future extensions — and what each would cost us

A research pass in August 2026 mapped what lies beyond the current design. None of it is counted in our success bars; each item earns its way in only when demonstrated. The first result was a validation: every commercial architecture that deviates from our design point — grids of small cores, reconfigurable fabrics, exposed datapaths — pays for its flexibility in measured efficiency. We are not leaving conventional gains on the table.

The nearer extensions are engineering rather than research. Our memory traffic is perfectly sequential, which permits many slow memory channels with short-reach interfaces — the same physics that makes on-package memory several times cheaper per bit than off-package. With memory now priced at roughly a fifth to a third of the energy budget, that is worth perhaps 10–40 % at system level. The internal memory organisation and the interconnect between compute arrays are specification gaps rather than open questions — though the interconnect is the one term that could subtract rather than add, and we will price it before trusting any array-size decision.

The furthest extension is three-dimensional integration: memory die bonded directly above the compute die, with intermediate values passed vertically between stacked compute tiers instead of through large on-chip memories. Real test silicon exists elsewhere at a tenth of the usual interface energy, and it is the only known approach that attacks the largest single energy category — moving operands around — head on. It is fully digital and bit-exact, and a shipped competitor cannot re-stack itself. Three questions gate it, and we will answer them at a desk before spending anything: whether stacked memory survives the temperatures of a fanless head, whether the capacity we need exists outside custom supply agreements, and whether hybrid bonds hold up to the vibration and thermal cycling of a machine that moves.

We call this level the frontier: 3D DRAM — the north star is the ceiling of today’s design space; the frontier is the expanded space, in escrow until the instrument can recompute it. The full model lives in hybrid-bonded DRAM stacked on the compute die: each weight read drops from roughly 40 to 8 picojoules per byte over short, wide vertical connections, with 8–16 GB of stacked DRAM over 100–200 MB of distributed SRAM and each memory bank feeding its own compute cluster, so weights never cross a central interface. Our energy ledger prices the cheaper reads at about 1.16× whole-system today (decomposed: ×1.11 from repricing the ∼12 % of chunk energy that is DRAM, and ×1.04 from shorter exposed-memory time feeding back into static energy); the full bank-local redesign targets 1.3–2×, an estimate. The energy number also understates the strategic value: at the current memory interface, any compute improvement — however radical — converts at most about 2× of additional efficiency into speed before the chunk becomes memory-bound. Stacked memory is what moves that ceiling, keeping future compute gains convertible into throughput. Because any overlap with the ceiling’s own overhead assumptions can only shrink the result, the product is a rigorous upper bound: an envelope of roughly 20–31× for the 3D-DRAM redesign and 29–44× compounded with the gated circuit techniques — bounds the recomputation cannot exceed, not predictions. Beyond it sits the horizon: model–hardware co-design — the model changing because the silicon changes with it — deliberately undefined, and not capped by the hardware-only envelope above.

On what these extensions would lock in: the near ones commit us to nothing about the model. Three-dimensional stacking deepens a commitment we have already made — the attention operation chain shared by every standard transformer, including both model families we serve. It would not serve a post-transformer architecture as gracefully as our current design would. That is a deeper version of an existing bet, not a new one, and we state it rather than discover it later.

11. Why this stays defensible

The socket is the humanoid head. You win it per robot generation, and you defend it with certification — deterministic timing, once it is inside somebody's safety case, is a moat measured in years rather than months.

It is worth being explicit about why we price the bet on the ratio rather than on absolute real-time. World models are getting smaller quickly — 2.3 B in LaWAM, 1 B in Efficient-WAM, 500 M in SmolVLA [X]. Absolute feasibility arrives eventually on its own, for us and for NVIDIA alike. What does not arrive on its own is energy per FLOP. Measured GPU performance per watt doubles roughly every three to four years, far slower than the headline throughput figure, and inside a fixed 40 W head you cannot spend your way past it [X]. η comes from deleting generality, and a process node is available to both parties, so the ratio survives what the absolute number does not.

Each competitor misses one constraint, and it is a different one each time. Thor stays general because CUDA is the product. Datacenter transformer ASICs are 20× off in power regime. Edge NPUs are roughly 100× too small for a 14 B streaming model. The relative bar, 2× Thor in-head, rides entirely on η. The absolute 5 Hz loop is a joint model-and-silicon program, and we say so in public rather than only in private.
