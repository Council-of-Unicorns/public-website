Business · draft B

The plan

Substrate is building the RPU — the Robot Processing Unit: an inference coprocessor and compiler for running large AI models on robots. Robots have a fixed power budget. The models we would like to run on them do not. Our job is to let a robotics team spend 20–40 watts on substantially more useful inference than it can get from a general-purpose edge GPU.

Our current pre-silicon work points to a roughly 3–4× class advantage over Jetson Thor on the large-transformer workloads we are studying: about 2.9× at first silicon and 4.2× as the compiler matures. We froze a 2× bar before exploring the design space and design toward 3×. Those are predictions, not silicon measurements — the simulation page shows exactly how they are built and what they assume. The next year is about finding out whether they survive contact with physics and customers.

What we are actually betting on

We are not building a DreamZero chip.

Physical-AI models are changing too quickly to make one architecture the basis of a multi-year silicon program. DreamZero is useful because it gives us a demanding reference workload. It is not a prediction of what every robot will run three years from now.

What looks more durable is the workload underneath it: large, batch-1 transformer inference under tight latency and power constraints — dense low-precision tensor work, attention over long context, big working sets with persistent state, and data movement predictable enough to schedule in advance. That is where we specialize first. The compiler gives us enough flexibility to follow the model frontier without paying all of the generality cost of a GPU.

In other words, RPU v1 is specialized around a class of computation, not one frozen neural network.

Why several times more compute matters

The point is not to make today’s model finish a benchmark faster.

The more interesting question is what a robotics team does if we give it three or four times more useful inference inside the same power envelope. It might use a larger model. It might process more cameras, retain more history, plan further ahead, or simply react faster.

The customer we want is the team telling us: “the model we actually want to deploy works better, but we cannot run it onboard at the latency and power we need.” If we can change that answer, the efficiency multiplier becomes robot capability rather than a benchmark number. That is the product hypothesis we are testing with customers — not a law of nature we are assuming.

Start as a coprocessor

We do not ask a robotics company to replace its entire compute stack.

The first RPU is a coprocessor. The robot keeps its Jetson or Qualcomm SoC for cameras, ROS, networking, safety, and general-purpose control. The expensive model workload moves to us. A customer gives us a PyTorch or JAX model; our software profiles it, maps it onto the RPU, schedules memory and compute, and produces a deployment. Anything we do not support can remain on the existing processor.

That gives us a narrow integration wedge: win the model workload first.

The simulator and compiler

We cannot know today which physical-AI architecture will win. What we can build is the machinery to evaluate each one quickly.

The simulator tells us where latency and energy go — and we made it earn that role: it reproduces our lab measurements of NVIDIA’s newest silicon to within 0.5–3.9 percent on execution time and 0.8–2.5 percent on energy before we trust it about hardware that does not exist yet. An independently built cycle-level model checks the geometry and scheduling assumptions against it. The compiler tells us whether a given architecture can actually extract the predicted performance.

When a new model appears, we want to answer in days rather than months: does the RPU still win, where does it win, and what should the next chip change? That capability matters before the first tapeout, and it becomes more valuable with every customer workload we see.

The next six months

The first priority is the team: recruit an exceptional ASIC/accelerator architect and a compiler/performance architect. Customer discovery runs in parallel.

Then we replace assumptions with evidence:

  • Run the sealed Orin experiment — we registered the predicted result before touching the hardware, so our first cross-architecture test is one we can lose.
  • Benchmark real Jetson Thor hardware at the target 40 watts on frozen workloads, replacing our estimates of the incumbent with measurements.
  • Physically characterize the critical RPU blocks — post-layout, PDK-based energy for the arithmetic and memory, not spreadsheet estimates.
  • Run a scaled RPU on an FPGA.
  • Put the first design-partner workloads through the compiler.

The philosophy behind the sequencing is simple: every stage should be capable of killing the company before the next stage becomes expensive. The first hard line is already set — if the physically characterized compute block shows less than a 2.2× advantage, just above our success bar, we stop.

Six to twelve months

The next phase is about proving that the pieces work together. We validate SRAM behavior, network traffic, DMA scheduling, and compiler activity against the FPGA. We run several plausible model families through the architecture rather than optimizing one benchmark forever.

We want two or three serious design partners before tapeout, with the gate stated plainly: hit this latency at these watts, support these operators, and we will evaluate the part. Evaluations, not purchase orders — we will say so when that changes.

Then we freeze RPU v1 and make the expensive decision: whether the evidence is strong enough to manufacture it.

Years two and three

If the pre-silicon evidence survives, year two is tapeout and compiler development against the FPGA and partner workloads. Year three is first silicon, bring-up, and the first serious customer integrations.

The objective of the first generation is not to build the final robot computer. It is to prove that specialization creates enough advantage to justify a new physical-AI compute platform.

What happens when the models converge

The first RPU is being designed while physical-AI architecture is still moving. That limits how specialized we can responsibly make it. We think that constraint eventually goes away.

If the industry settles around a more stable architecture for perception, world modeling, memory, planning, and action, we can design the neural network and the processor together instead of optimizing them independently. Memory can be organized around the model’s actual reuse patterns. Communication can follow its actual graph. Repeated operations can become dedicated pipelines. Precision and programmability can exist only where the model needs them. And the model itself can change, because the silicon changes with it.

Our first-generation thesis is a roughly 3–4× class hardware advantage. The long-term ambition is to find out whether deep model–hardware co-design can turn that into a much larger end-to-end advantage — approaching an order of magnitude on important physical-AI workloads. The simulation page bounds it at 15.7× with every input at its favorable end, 22× if gated low-voltage circuit techniques prove out. That bound is our north star: not part of the underwriting case for RPU v1, but the outcome the machinery is built to chase. Beyond it sit two more levels. The optimized RPU: the chip rebuilt around 3D DRAM — the whole model resident in memory stacked on the compute die — with an upper-bound envelope of roughly 20–44×; a bound, not a plan. And codesign: the model and the silicon designed together — a planning band of roughly 7–19×, an estimate.

We do not depend on that outcome for RPU v1. It is the upside if the architecture converges and we are already the team with the compiler, the customer workloads, the simulator, and the silicon experience required to build around it. That is why we want to enter before convergence rather than wait for it.

The two things that can make us wrong

There are two ways this company can be wrong.

The first is technical. If physical design and real Thor measurements pull the advantage down toward 1.3×, a new hardware platform probably is not worth the integration cost. That is what the staged spending and the 2.2× line exist to catch early.

The second is that physical-AI inference stops being compute hungry. Models may become small and efficient enough that general-purpose edge GPUs have abundant headroom. We do not know that this cannot happen. Our bet is that frontier robotics will keep finding valuable uses for additional inference — whether that comes from larger models, more visual input, longer memory, world modeling, planning, or test-time computation. We will test that assumption with customers and with every new model family we run through the simulator.

Where this goes

The first RPU is a specialized processor for large physical-AI transformers at the edge. The larger company is a hardware–software co-design platform for embodied intelligence.

Before the model architecture converges, our job is to stay several times ahead of general-purpose edge GPUs without betting on the wrong model. When it does converge, we want to be in position to build the processor around the model itself.

RPU v1 is about making today’s frontier models practical at the edge. The long-term opportunity is to build the hardware around whatever physical intelligence becomes.
